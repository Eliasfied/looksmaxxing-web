Look at your side profile and your eye goes to where the bottom of your nose meets your upper lip. That junction has a name and a measurement. The **nasolabial angle** turns up in rhinoplasty consultations, orthodontic treatment plans and a lot of online profile threads.

The number is real and clinically useful. It is also more slippery than most posts admit. This guide covers what the angle measures, why sources give different "normal" ranges, how to estimate yours from a photo, and what you can and can't do with the result.

## What the nasolabial angle actually is

The nasolabial angle (often shortened to **NLA**) is the angle between the base of the nose and the upper lip, seen in profile. More precisely, it is measured at the junction between the **columella** and the upper lip. The columella is the strip of tissue that separates your nostrils.

Three landmarks define it:

- **Columella line:** a line along the underside of the nose, from the tip toward the face.
- **Subnasale:** the point where the columella meets the upper lip. This is the vertex, or corner, of the angle.
- **Upper lip line:** a line running down the front of the upper lip.

The angle between those two lines is your NLA.

- A **smaller (more acute)** angle means the nose tip points more downward relative to the lip.
- A **larger (more obtuse)** angle means the tip sits more upturned, or the lip sits further back.

Rhinoplasty surgeons use the angle as a shorthand for **tip rotation**, which is how far the nose tip is turned up or down. In surgical education material, an angle sharper than the surgeon's reference range is described as an *under-rotated* tip. That gives a drooping or hooked look in profile.

## Why "normal" nasolabial angle ranges disagree

Any article that gives one ideal number without context is oversimplifying. The research literature itself says the data on the mean ideal value are controversial.

### The commonly cited ranges

The broadest figure you will see is **90 to 120 degrees**. A cross-sectional cephalometric study notes that this range is widely quoted and that it varies with sex and ethnicity. Narrower ranges also circulate:

- One frequently cited reference puts the ideal at 100 to 105 degrees for women and 90 to 95 degrees for men.
- Another study reported an average of 102 degrees, plus or minus 8.
- A third reported 105 degrees, plus or minus 13, for women and 107.6 degrees, plus or minus 8.5, for men.

Look at that last pair. In that sample, men averaged slightly *higher* than women. That runs against the common claim that women should always have the more obtuse angle. A review of these values sits in this [observational cohort study of orthodontic patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11763396/).

### Sex, ethnicity and age

Groups differ, so a single universal target doesn't fit everyone. Age also matters. In a regression study of a Peruvian sample, age had a statistically significant effect on the angle. A value that is typical at 20 may not be typical at 50, and comparing yourself with someone of a different age or background is not like for like.

### Measurement method

This is the part most online content skips. Researchers have pointed out that **how the subnasale point is located** can change the result. Choosing a slightly different tangent line along a curved columella or a curved lip also changes the number. Some authors call measurement method the main reason studies report different values.

There is some reassurance here. In the orthodontic cohort above, angles measured on standardized photographs and on lateral X-rays were not significantly different. So a careful photo can approximate a clinical measurement. A careless one cannot.

## How to estimate your nasolabial angle from a photo

This gives you a rough estimate, not a clinical measurement. Use it to understand your profile, not to diagnose anything.

1. **Shoot a true side profile.** Put the camera at face height, about 1.5 to 2 metres away, and zoom slightly rather than standing close. Wide-angle lenses at close range distort the nose.
2. **Keep your head level.** Look straight ahead at a point on the wall. Tilting the chin up or down changes how both the nose and the lip line read.
3. **Relax your lips.** Close them gently with no pursing, smiling or pressing. Lip posture moves the upper lip line.
4. **Find subnasale.** Zoom in and mark the deepest point where the underside of the nose turns into the upper lip.
5. **Draw the two lines.** Draw one along the underside of the columella and one along the front of the upper lip. Use any photo editor or free protractor app.
6. **Read the angle, then repeat.** Take three photos on different days and measure each one. If your readings spread by several degrees, that tells you how much of the number comes from method rather than anatomy.

## What the number can't tell you

The NLA is formed by **two separate structures**: the nose above and the upper lip below. One number cannot tell you which side produced it.

Two people can have the same 95-degree angle for opposite reasons:

- **Person A** has a nose tip that angles downward and a fairly upright upper lip.
- **Person B** has a neutral nose tip and an upper lip that slopes forward over prominent front teeth.

These are different situations, and a clinician would think about them differently. This is why the angle is never used alone in a professional assessment. It sits alongside tip projection, lip position, tooth position, and the relationship between upper and lower jaws.

The angle also tells you nothing about overall attractiveness. Treat an ideal number as a reference point that clinicians use for planning, not as a grade.

## Where clinicians use the nasolabial angle

The NLA comes up most often in two specialties. Here is what the research shows about each. None of it is a recommendation.

### Rhinoplasty and tip rotation

We don't recommend rhinoplasty for appearance. It is surgery. The main risks are the general risks of anesthesia, bleeding and infection, possible effects on nasal breathing, and results that shift during healing, sometimes leading to revision surgery. *Talk to a qualified professional before considering this.*

The research on how the angle behaves after surgery shows why that shifting matters:

- In a prospective study of 25 patients, the mean angle was 91.44 degrees before surgery, 108.84 degrees immediately after, and **97.2 degrees at three months**. The authors noted that the angle can drop in the months after surgery ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33936867/)).
- In a prospective study of 111 rhinoplasty patients, the largest change in the angle was about 9.3 degrees of elevation one week after surgery. The authors also argued that measurements of tip rotation are inconsistent across the literature, and that a standardized measure is needed.
- A separate 12-month follow-up study reported that the angle decreased over time after surgery.

In short, the angle someone sees in the mirror shortly after surgery is not a reliable preview of the long-term result.

### Orthodontics and lip position

Orthodontic treatment, including tooth extraction, has genuine dental uses, such as crowding and bite problems. Whether to extract teeth is a decision for an orthodontist based on dental health and function, not on a profile angle.

The NLA does show up as a side effect worth understanding:

- A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1532338217302439) found a significant increase in the nasolabial angle and lip retraction in extraction cases. That is consistent with a flatter profile. The authors noted that whether these changes are beneficial or detrimental depends on other factors.
- A retrospective study of high-angle Class II patients reported an average increase of 4.39 degrees, with a standard deviation of 8.09. A spread that wide means individual results varied a lot.
- For teenagers, a recent opinion article stressed that clinicians need to separate treatment effects from normal soft tissue maturation, because the profile is still developing.

If you are in orthodontic treatment and care about your profile, ask your orthodontist how the plan is expected to affect lip position. It is a fair question, and they are best placed to answer it.

## Practical things that affect how your profile reads

You can't groom your way to a different skeletal angle. You can control how this area comes across in photos and in person. The points below are editorial suggestions, not measured effects on the angle itself.

### Photo setup

- **Use a longer focal length.** Close-up phone selfies exaggerate the nose and compress the lip area. Step back and zoom in.
- **Keep your head neutral.** A slightly raised chin reads very differently in profile from a dropped one. Choose deliberately rather than by accident.
- **Use side light from the front.** Light from slightly in front of your face softens shadows under the nose. Harsh overhead light deepens the shadow at subnasale.

### Resting lip posture

People often press their lips together or push them forward without noticing, especially when posing. Practise a relaxed, closed-lip rest position in the mirror so the upper lip line in photos matches how you look in daily life.

### Grooming around the upper lip

For people who grow facial hair, a moustache sits directly over the lip half of this angle and changes how the area reads visually. That can mean a neat trimmed line, a fuller style, or clean-shaven. Experiment, take profile photos of each option, and compare them side by side.

## Myths to skip

**"There is one perfect nasolabial angle."** The literature disagrees on the ideal, and values vary with sex, ethnicity, age and measurement method. A range is more honest than a single number.

**"Women should always have a much higher angle than men."** Some references suggest this. At least one dataset found men averaging slightly higher. Treat sex-based targets as loose conventions.

**"You can change your nasolabial angle with exercises or tongue posture."** We found no peer-reviewed evidence that habits like mewing change this angle. Be skeptical of anyone who promises it.

**"Nasolabial angle and nasolabial folds are the same thing."** They are not. Nasolabial folds are the creases running from the sides of the nose to the corners of the mouth. The nasolabial angle is the profile angle between the underside of the nose and the upper lip.

**"A screenshot measurement is accurate to the degree."** Small shifts in landmark placement, head tilt and lens distortion can move the reading. Measure several photos and look at the spread before trusting any single result.

## The takeaway

The nasolabial angle describes how the underside of the nose meets the upper lip. Clinicians find it useful for planning, and it is easy to misread on your own.

Use it to understand your profile. Remember that both the nose and the lip feed into it, and that "normal" depends on who is measuring and how. If something about your nose or bite genuinely bothers you, a qualified professional can assess it properly. For everything else, photo setup, lip posture and grooming are within your control today.
