If you have watched a Qoves Studio video, you know the style. The videos are dense, citation-heavy breakdowns of why some faces read the way they do. The paid report promises to apply that same analysis to *your* face. That is an appealing pitch, and it costs real money, so it deserves a careful look.

This Qoves review covers the price, what the report contains, what buyers say, the strongest criticism, and how to decide whether it fits your goal.

**A note on sources.** Qoves shows pricing only at checkout, so the figures below come from third-party reviews of the checkout page. Several of those reviewers sell competing face-analysis tools. So does Aura, which publishes this article. We link every claim to its source so you can check it yourself, and you should confirm the price on the Qoves checkout page before buying.

## Qoves Review at a Glance

- **What it is:** a human-reviewed facial analysis report plus a personalised improvement protocol.
- **Price:** $150 per year for one analysis, with optional add-ons that can bring the total to $435.
- **Turnaround:** up to 28 days as standard, or faster for an extra fee.
- **Refunds:** reportedly only before you submit your photos or questionnaire.
- **Best fit:** someone who wants a detailed written assessment to bring to a professional consultation.
- **Main concern:** academics have questioned whether attractiveness research supports personalised recommendations at all.

## What Qoves Studio Is

Qoves started as a YouTube channel. A [SoftMaxx review](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-review) describes it as a research-driven channel that broke down facial aesthetics with citations and anthropometric reasoning. *Anthropometry* is the measurement of body and facial dimensions, such as the distance between the eyes or the ratio of face width to height.

The paid product grew out of that reputation. The same review describes it as a facial analysis compiled with human review, plus a protocol of recommendations. This sets Qoves apart from instant selfie-scoring apps. A person is involved in preparing your report, which is part of why delivery takes weeks rather than seconds.

The packaging has changed over time. [Real World Appeal](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-facial-analysis-cost) notes that earlier buyers described tiered one-time reports ranging from roughly $100 to $300. The current product is sold as a yearly membership. If you read an older review online, its pricing may not match what you see today.

### No public price list

[Real World Appeal](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-facial-analysis-cost) points out that Qoves publishes no public price list, so the checkout page is the only source of truth. Treat every number above, including ours, as a snapshot.

According to [Real World Appeal's review](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-review), refunds are only available *before* you submit your photos or questionnaire. The same review notes that "cancel anytime" stops the next renewal but does not refund the current year. Uploading your photos is the point of no return, so make your decision before that step, not after.

## What Is Inside a Qoves Report

The report has three layers: measurement, interpretation, and recommendations. It helps to read each one differently.

### The measurement layer

Qoves maps landmarks on your face and runs them through a set of assessments. [Real World Appeal](https://realworldappeal.com/en/blog/qoves-review) notes that the Qoves homepage cites 521 landmark points, its FAQ cites 512, and both are run through "160+" beauty markers. The mismatch is minor, but it is a reminder that these are marketing figures, not independently audited ones.

A Qoves [sample report](https://www.scribd.com/document/683724839/Sample-FINAL-Comprehensive-Report-V2) describes its method as a *cephalometric* approach. Cephalometry is the measurement of the head and face. It comes from orthodontics, where it is traditionally done on X-rays. Here, it means soft-tissue measurements, taken from the skin surface in photos, which are then compared with published research data.

The same sample report says most of that research data concerns 'ideal' values as they correspond to a 1 to 10 scale of attractiveness rated by laypeople. That detail is easy to skim past. It means the benchmark your face is compared against comes from average ratings given by groups of ordinary people in specific studies.

### The protocol and visualisations

The membership includes a personalised improvement protocol and before-and-after visualisations. [SoftMaxx](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-review) makes an important clarification: the visualisations are projections made from your own photos, not results. An edited image shows a possibility. It is not evidence that any step in the protocol will produce that look.

### What it is like to read

One buyer who [reviewed her Qoves analysis on Medium](https://alessaweiler.medium.com/my-honest-qoves-review-i-tried-their-aesthetic-analysis-these-are-my-results-be7aa6d9b61f) described an assessments section covering how feminine she looked and how proportional and symmetric her face was. A separate analysis section broke each part of her face into components, with statistics on each one's contribution to her overall look. She liked it, but also admitted there was a bit of information overload. Expect a lot of reading, and set aside time to work through it.

## What Buyers Say

The handful of public reviews we looked at point in different directions. They are too few to show an overall trend, so treat them as examples rather than a verdict.

- **Praise for depth.** One review on [Trustpilot](https://nz.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com) called the level of detail, care and professionalism in the report exceptional.
- **Slower than promised at times.** A review on the same Trustpilot page noted that turnaround took longer than the website stated because of high demand.
- **Some disappointment.** Another [Trustpilot reviewer](https://fi.trustpilot.com/review/qoves.com) called the service "highly disappointing" and took issue with the example photo Qoves shows.
- **Questions about value and the end goal.** The writer behind [Uncynical](https://uncynical.substack.com/p/i-tried-qoves-which-isnt-a-looksmaxxing) said they went in thinking Qoves was charging $250 for something they could build in about two hours with an AI coding tool. That figure is higher than the $150 base price cited elsewhere in this review, and the post does not explain the difference, so confirm the current price at checkout. After seeing their "after" picture, the writer was not keen on actually looking like that.

That last point is worth sitting with. A report can be detailed and well made, and you can still disagree with where it points you. Your preferences count.

Keep in mind that review platforms are self-selecting. Happy customers and frustrated ones are both more likely to post than people who felt neutral.

## The Criticism Worth Taking Seriously

The most substantial critique concerns the science rather than the service.

A [summary by The Face Analysis](https://thefaceanalysis.com/guides/qoves-review) describes a Guardian feature on paid facial analysis services. It reports three points:

1. A psychology professor whose research Qoves referenced in a video said she studies bullying and mental health, not beauty, and she raised a warning about such services.
2. Another academic who studies attraction said the literature is too inconsistent for population-wide generalisations.
3. Qoves did not respond to the Guardian's request for comment.

That summary comes from a competing site, so read the original Guardian piece if you want the full context.

There is also an older criticism. According to [EverybodyWiki](https://en.everybodywiki.com/QOVES_Studio), the facial assessment tool came under fire in 2020 for promoting Eurocentric beauty standards. EverybodyWiki is a low-reliability source, so treat this as a reported criticism rather than a settled finding.

### Measurement is not the same as judgment

Here is a simple way to use this criticism. A measurement, such as the width of your face relative to its height, describes your face. A judgment, such as calling that ratio good or bad, compares it with a benchmark. As the sample report itself describes, that benchmark comes from layperson ratings in research studies. Your face is not a population average, and your goals are not a survey result.

### About the surgical add-on

The surgical recommendations add-on suggests procedures and shows visualisations of them. A visualisation tells you nothing about risk, recovery, cost, or whether you are a suitable candidate. **Talk to a qualified professional before considering this.**

## Who Qoves Fits, and Who Should Skip It

**Qoves is a reasonable fit if you:**

- Are preparing for a dermatology or surgical consultation and want organised notes to bring with you. [SoftMaxx](https://softmaxx.io/blog/qoves-review) names this as the buyer it suits best.
- Enjoy long, detailed written analysis and will actually read it.
- Can wait up to four weeks, or are willing to pay for express delivery.
- Are comfortable with a yearly membership that renews.

**Consider skipping it if you:**

- Are going through body image distress. A detailed critique of your face can be hard to read, and the academics quoted above questioned the science behind these reports.
- Want to measure, change something, and re-check often. SoftMaxx notes that one report a year is the wrong cadence for that.
- Mainly want quick, practical styling direction, such as a haircut, grooming routine, or glasses shape.

## Practical Tips If You Decide to Buy Qoves

These are editorial suggestions, not instructions from Qoves.

1. **Confirm the price at checkout.** Note the base price, the add-on costs, and the renewal terms.
2. **Choose add-ons before you upload.** Because refunds reportedly end at photo submission, settle on express delivery or the surgical add-on first. Only then upload.
3. **Take consistent photos.** Use even, front-facing light, a neutral expression, and the same distance for every shot. Pull hair away from your face if the instructions ask for it. Inconsistent photos make any measurement less reliable.
4. **Read the protocol for what you control.** Start with low-risk items like grooming, skincare consistency, and hairstyle before thinking about anything invasive.
5. **Treat scores as context, not verdicts.** A low number on one marker describes a comparison to a study benchmark. It does not describe your worth or how others see you day to day.
6. **Bring the report to a professional.** If anything in it involves treatments or procedures, use it as a list of questions for a qualified clinician rather than a plan to act on alone.
7. **Set a renewal reminder.** Put a calendar note a few weeks before the membership renews, so you can decide whether a second year is worth it.
8. **Notice how it makes you feel.** If reading the report leaves you anxious or fixated on a feature, step away and talk to someone you trust or a mental health professional.

## The Bottom Line

Qoves sells a detailed, human-reviewed facial report built on a measurement-heavy method. Some buyers praise its depth. The fair criticisms are its cost over time, an early refund cutoff, and a scientific foundation that academics have called too inconsistent for personalised recommendations. Use it as organised homework for a professional conversation, not as an objective ranking of your face, and it can earn its place. If your real goal is better styling, start with something smaller and cheaper.
