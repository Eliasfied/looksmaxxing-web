Facial thirds come up in almost every conversation about facial harmony. Forums treat a 1:1:1 split as the mark of a balanced face. Surgeons sketch the three zones on patient photos. Face analysis apps report your ratios to two decimal places.

The idea has real history and real clinical use. It also has a weak spot that rarely gets mentioned. When researchers measure actual people, including people others rate as attractive, the thirds are usually *not* equal.

This guide covers what the thirds are, where the rule came from, and what measured faces show. It then explains how to take your own measurements and read them without turning a rough guideline into a verdict on your face.

## What facial thirds are

Facial thirds divide the front of the face into three stacked horizontal zones, from the hairline down to the chin. Each zone is bounded by an anatomical landmark. A landmark is a point clinicians can find the same way on any face.

The standard landmarks used in facial plastic surgery texts are:

- **Trichion:** the midpoint of the hairline at the top of the forehead.
- **Glabella:** the smooth, slightly raised point between the eyebrows.
- **Subnasale:** the point where the base of the nose meets the upper lip.
- **Menton:** the lowest point of the chin.

These four points create three zones:

1. **Upper third:** trichion to glabella. This zone is mostly forehead.
2. **Middle third:** glabella to subnasale. This zone covers the eyes, cheekbones and nose.
3. **Lower third:** subnasale to menton. This zone covers the lips, chin and lower jaw.

### The lower third has its own thirds

Clinicians often split the lower face again. In a common reference version, the upper lip (subnasale to **stomion**, the point where the lips meet) takes up one-third of the lower face. The lower lip and chin take up the remaining two-thirds. A facial plastic surgery reference text on preoperative evaluation lists both the main thirds and this subdivision.

The practical value is that it breaks a vague impression like "my chin looks small" into a specific comparison you can actually check.

## Where the equal-thirds rule came from

The idea that the three zones should be equal comes from the **neoclassical canons**. These are proportion rules from classical art that Renaissance artists later revived. A review published in Head & Face Medicine notes that [Leonardo da Vinci described body and facial canons in the late 1400s](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4369102/). The same paper credits Leslie Farkas, called the father of modern facial anthropometry, with going back to these canons in the 1980s. **Anthropometry** is the measurement of the human body. Farkas did what the artists had not: he measured real people against the canons.

The equal-thirds rule still appears in clinical texts. One plastic surgery facial analysis chapter states that the face can be divided into equal thirds with the nose occupying the middle third. The same text recommends analyzing the upper third first, then the middle, then the lower.

That is the most useful way to see the rule. It is a **framework for looking at the face one region at a time**. It is not a measured description of what faces are like.

## What measurements of real faces show

This is where most online content stops short. Researchers have tested the equal-thirds canon on many populations, and it often fails.

### Equal thirds are uncommon

- A chapter on facial proportions states plainly that the facial thirds are rarely equal. It describes different typical patterns in different groups. In Caucasians, the middle third is often shorter than the upper third, and both are shorter than the lower third. In East Asians, the middle third is often longer than the upper third and equal to the lower third.
- A study of Southern Chinese young adults published in PLOS ONE found that [the facial three-section canon did not apply at all](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0052593). The authors recommended judging faces against norms specific to ethnicity and gender instead of the classical ideal.
- In young adults from the Arabian Peninsula, the equal-thirds canon was not confirmed. The authors noted that their subjects were aesthetically pleasing even though they did not have equal thirds.
- In a study of Tibetan students, none fully matched the three-section canon, and the upper third was usually the longest.
- In a study of young Nigerian women, the average middle third (61.04 mm) was significantly shorter than the upper (73.10 mm) and lower (75.49 mm) thirds. The authors concluded that faces can depart from ideal proportions and still be perceived as pleasing.
- A study of Turkish adults found the neoclassical canons invalid for most of its sample.

### The evidence on the other side

To be fair, the picture is not entirely one-sided. A study comparing attractive and anonymous women found that the attractive group showed [more uniform facial thirds and fifths](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3951104/), with no statistical difference between their thirds. A study of 50 Caucasian models concluded that some neoclassical canons still hold while others seem to have changed over time.

### What to take from this

Facial thirds are a fair way to describe a face. They are not a fair way to rank one. Relatively equal thirds appear in some samples of attractive faces. Unequal thirds are normal, and they are common in faces people find attractive. Typical patterns also differ between populations. A forehead that is longer than your lower face is not, on its own, a flaw.

## How to measure your own facial thirds

If you want your own numbers, the photo matters more than the math. Small changes in camera position move the landmarks.

1. **Shoot straight on at eye level.** Prop the phone on a shelf or tripod level with your eyes. A camera held below the chin stretches the lower face. A camera held above the head stretches the forehead.
2. **Step back.** Wide-angle phone lenses distort faces at arm's length. Have someone take the photo from a few steps away, or use a timer and a slight zoom. *(Editorial suggestion.)*
3. **Keep your head level.** Look straight into the lens with your chin neither tucked nor raised. Tilting the head changes how long each third looks in a flat photo.
4. **Show your hairline.** Pull hair back so the trichion is visible. If your hairline is receding or irregular, note that your upper third will read longer. That says something about your hair, not your bone structure.
5. **Use a neutral expression.** Keep the mouth closed and relaxed with the teeth lightly together. Smiling lifts the cheeks and changes the lower face.
6. **Mark the four landmarks.** Open the photo in any editor and note the vertical position of the trichion, glabella, subnasale and menton.
7. **Compare the three distances.** Divide each third by the largest one, or by the total height, to get proportions. Units don't matter for ratios, so pixels are fine.

As an illustrative example, a face might measure 6.0, 6.2 and 6.8 units from top to bottom. The lower third is the longest. Given the studies above, that is an ordinary result, not a problem to solve.

## How to read your results

The numbers tell you *where* your face carries its length. They do not tell you how attractive you are. A few rules of thumb for interpreting them, as editorial guidance:

- **Compare your result to similar faces, not to 1:1:1.** The research above shows typical patterns differ by population and by sex, so a classical ideal is a poor benchmark.
- **Treat small differences as noise.** Hand-placed landmarks on a phone photo are not precise. Retake the photo and remeasure before drawing any conclusion.
- **Check what is soft tissue and what is styling.** Hairline position, eyebrow grooming, beard shape and lip fullness all shift where the eye reads the boundaries.
- **Look at the whole face.** Thirds describe vertical length only. Width, symmetry, skin and expression all shape how a face comes across.

## Styling around your proportions

You can't change bone length, but you can change how each zone *reads*. The suggestions below are editorial styling judgments, not measured effects. They apply to anyone, whatever look you're going for.

### Upper third (forehead)

- **If your upper third reads long:** a fringe, curtain bangs, a textured crop or hair brought forward visually shortens the forehead. Side parts break up a large vertical surface more than a severe center part does.
- **If your upper third reads short:** pushed-back or lifted styles, off-the-face cuts and fuller volume on top add visual height.
- **Brows sit on the boundary.** Well-groomed brows sharpen the line between the upper and middle thirds. Very thin or very low brows can make the forehead look longer.

### Middle third (eyes, cheeks, nose)

- **Glasses frames** sit across this zone. Heavier or deeper frames draw attention to the middle of the face. Lighter or rimless frames keep the eye moving.
- **Makeup**, for those who wear it, can shift emphasis. Blush placed higher on the cheek or contour along the cheekbone changes how this zone reads.

### Lower third (lips, chin, jaw)

- **Beards** change the outline of the lower third more than anything else. A fuller beard at the chin adds length. A beard kept fuller at the sides and shorter at the chin adds width and makes the lower face read shorter.
- **Lip color and liner**, for those who wear them, can shift balance within the lower third's own one-third and two-thirds split.
- **Body fat and posture** affect jawline definition and how the chin sits in photos. A forward head posture blurs the chin and neck line.

## Where procedures fit

Online threads about facial thirds often end in talk of chin implants, fillers or jaw surgery to "fix" a ratio. **We don't recommend surgery, implants, fillers or other injectables for appearance.** Their main risks include infection, nerve injury and numbness, asymmetry, implant or filler migration, and a real chance of needing revision procedures. They are also built on the premise this article questions: that equal thirds are a target worth chasing.

The non-medical options covered above come first: hairstyle, grooming, beard shape, posture and body fat. Bite problems are different. A study of patients with Class II division 1 malocclusion, an overbite pattern, found their proportions deviated most in the lower third. If you have trouble chewing, jaw pain or bite issues, that is a medical and dental matter. Talk to a qualified professional, such as a dentist or orthodontist, before considering any treatment. Decide it based on function and their assessment, not on your ratios.

## The short version

- Facial thirds split the face at the hairline, the point between the brows, the base of the nose and the bottom of the chin.
- The equal-thirds rule comes from classical art. It is a useful way to look at the face region by region.
- Measured populations rarely have equal thirds, and attractive faces often don't either. Typical patterns vary by ethnicity and sex.
- Measure from a level, straight-on photo taken at a distance, and treat small differences as noise.
- Use the results to guide hair, grooming and styling choices, not to judge your face.
