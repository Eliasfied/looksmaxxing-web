If you searched for how to fix hooded eyes, you probably want your eyes to look more open in the mirror and in photos. That's a reasonable goal. Many guides jump straight to eyeliner hacks or surgery without asking the more useful question: *what is actually creating the hood on your eyes?*

The answer changes everything. A fold of skin from a lower brow behaves differently from loose lid skin, and both are different from an eyelid that sits too low. This guide walks you through telling them apart. It then covers practical, low-risk ways to change how your eyes read: makeup placement, brow grooming, lashes and photo angles. It also explains when hooding stops being a styling question and becomes a medical one.

One honest note up front. Nothing in a makeup bag or grooming routine removes skin. These techniques change how the eye *reads*, not its structure. For most people, that's enough.

## What hooded eyes actually are

[Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/hooded-eyes) describes hooded eyelids as excess skin that folds down from the brow line. People tend to link hooding with aging, but genetics can play a role too. Some people have this lid shape for most of their lives.

As a description, "hooded eyes" is usually just an eye shape. One oculoplastic surgeon's overview points out that people often use the term for a visible shape without having any diagnosed condition. If you've always had this lid shape, nothing is wrong with your eyes.

### Three structures that can create the hood

The same hooded look can come from different structures. That same surgeon's overview separates terms that are easy to confuse:

- **Dermatochalasis**: excess or lax skin on the eyelid itself. The skin folds over the crease.
- **Brow ptosis**: a lowered eyebrow. When the brow sits lower, it can shift skin and soft tissue down onto the lid.
- **Blepharoptosis** (often just called *ptosis*): the edge of the upper eyelid sits too low, sometimes covering part of the pupil.

You can have one of these, two, or all three at once. That's why one-size-fits-all fixes disappoint so many people.

### How aging changes the picture

Medical News Today explains the aging pathway this way: as the eyebrows droop, they press down on the soft tissue and fat covering the eye socket, which creates the hooded effect. It also mentions less common causes, including trauma to the side of the head and some medical treatments.

The practical takeaway is that hooding you've noticed developing over years often involves the brow, not just the lid.

## Step one: figure out what is causing your hood

Before you change anything, spend five minutes getting a rough read on your own eyes. This is an editorial suggestion for self-observation, not a diagnosis.

1. **Use even, front-facing light.** Stand facing a window or a soft light at eye level. Overhead bathroom light casts shadows under the brow that exaggerate hooding.
2. **Relax your forehead completely.** Many people with hooded eyes raise their brows without noticing. Close your eyes, let your forehead go slack, then open your eyes without lifting your brows.
3. **Look straight ahead.** Notice how much of your mobile lid, the part that moves when you blink, is visible.
4. **Gently support the brow with a fingertip.** Lift the outer brow a few millimetres without stretching the lid skin. If much of the hood disappears, the brow is likely a big contributor. If a clear fold remains on the lid, loose lid skin is probably playing a role.
5. **Check the lid edge, not just the fold.** Look at where the lash line sits against your iris and pupil. If the edge of the lid covers part of your pupil, that's not ordinary hooding. Read the next section.
6. **Take a reference photo.** Shoot straight on, at eye level, in the same light. You'll use it to judge whether styling changes help.

A surgeon's article that cites the American Academy of Ophthalmology's EyeWiki makes a useful point here. A low brow can look a lot like excess eyelid skin, which is why brow position matters. Many people who think they have "too much lid skin" are really looking at a brow that sits lower than it used to.

## When hooded eyes are a medical question

For most people, hooding is purely cosmetic. Medical News Today notes that most people with hooded eyes don't need medical attention unless they can't see clearly, or the appearance is causing them real distress.

See a doctor, ideally an eye doctor, if you notice any of these:

- **Blocked vision.** You tilt your head back or raise your brows to see, or the top of your field of view feels cut off.
- **Fast change.** One clinic's guide advises seeing a physician if an eyelid changes quickly or your vision gets blocked.
- **The lid edge covers your pupil.** That points toward ptosis, which can have medical causes.
- **One side changes noticeably and the other doesn't,** especially alongside other symptoms.

These are reasons to get checked, not reasons to panic. A doctor can tell you which structure is involved, something a mirror can't do reliably.

## How to make hooded eyes look more open with makeup

Makeup is the lowest-risk way to change how hooded eyes read. The core problem is simple. When your eyes are open, the fold hides much of the lid, so product placed there disappears. Everything below works around that.

### A step-by-step routine

1. **Prep with primer.** A makeup artist quoted in Marie Claire UK starts with a good eyeshadow primer. Hooded lids touch the skin above them, so shadow tends to transfer and crease without one.
2. **Apply with your eyes open.** One beauty guide stresses applying makeup with your eyes open so you can see what stays visible beneath the hood. Do the placement this way, then refine.
3. **Place matte shadow slightly above the natural crease.** The same Marie Claire artist puts a matte shade just above the crease. Shadow sitting directly in the fold vanishes when your eyes open. Placing it a little higher creates visible depth where the eye actually shows.
4. **Keep liner thin and tight to the lashes.** The artist's do's and don'ts list says to keep eyeliner thin and to avoid thick, heavy liner across the lid. A thick line eats the small amount of visible lid you have.
5. **Lift the outer corner slightly.** *Editorial suggestion:* a short, upward flick at the outer edge, drawn with eyes open, helps the line stay visible instead of folding into the hood.
6. **Curl your lashes.** *Editorial suggestion:* curled lashes bring definition upward toward the visible part of the eye. Focus mascara at the roots.
7. **Use shimmer only where it shows.** *Editorial suggestion:* a touch of light-catching shade at the center of the lid or inner corner reads well. Shimmer across the whole hood tends to emphasize the fold.

### The thick-versus-thin liner disagreement

Not every expert agrees. Most guides say thin, but an Asian-American makeup artist interviewed by AOL recommends liner *thicker* than usual, because extra skin can hide a thin line once the eyes are open.

The reasonable answer is that it depends on how much lid shows. If almost none is visible when you look straight ahead, a thin line may disappear entirely, and a slightly bolder or smudged line may read better. If you have a sliver of visible lid, thin usually keeps it. Test both in your eyes-open mirror check.

One writer with hooded eyes sums it up in her Substack: with hooded lids, "it's all about the placement." That's personal experience, not science, but it matches what the professional advice above points to.

## Styling beyond makeup: brows, lashes and grooming

Not everyone wears makeup, and you don't need to. Several no-makeup changes affect how open the eye area looks. All of these are editorial suggestions, not measured effects.

- **Groom the brows from underneath.** Stray hairs below the brow line visually lower the brow and add weight above the lid. Tidying them keeps the brow shape cleaner and the space above the eye clearer. Avoid over-plucking the arch, which can make brows look thinner and lower over time.
- **Brush brows upward.** A clear or tinted brow gel set upward makes the brow look fuller and lighter. It doesn't change brow position, but it changes how heavy the area reads.
- **Choose glasses frames thoughtfully.** If you wear glasses, a frame whose top line sits near your brow line tends to look cleaner than a heavy bar cutting across the hood. Try frames on and judge from your eyes-open reference angle.
- **Watch the habitual brow lift.** Constantly raising your brows to "open" your eyes looks tense in photos. A relaxed face with good lighting usually looks better.
- **Compare on similar days.** Lids can look puffier on some mornings than others. Judge any change against photos taken in the same light, not against your best or worst day.

## Photos, camera angle and how eye shape reads

A lot of frustration with hooded eyes comes from photos. Camera position changes how the lid and brow read more than people expect:

- **A camera below eye level** looks up into the brow and makes the hood look heavier.
- **A camera slightly above eye level** shows more of the lid surface.
- **Overhead light** shadows the area under the brow. Front light from a window softens it.
- **A tilted head** changes how the outer corners look, which can make your eyes seem to angle up or down when they don't.

That last point connects hooding to a separate trait: *canthal tilt*, the apparent angle between the inner and outer corners of the eye. A heavy hood near the outer corner can change how that corner reads in a photo, and camera roll or head pose can distort it further. ## What about surgery, Botox or fillers?

You'll see eyelid surgery (*blepharoplasty*), brow lifts, Botox brow lifts, dermal fillers and laser treatments all over search results for this topic. **We don't recommend surgery, Botox, fillers or other cosmetic procedures to change how your eyes look.** Here's what these options are and their main drawbacks, so you can read other content critically.

- **They target different structures, and getting it wrong backfires.** The [American Society of Plastic Surgeons](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/articles/surgical-options-for-hooded-eyes-minimal-to-dramatic) describes upper blepharoplasty as an option when hooding comes from excess skin, and brow elevation when it comes from a descended brow. A surgeon's article warns that removing lid skin when the brow has dropped can pull the brow even lower and leave the eye looking crowded.
- **Botox can't remove skin.** One clinic's guide states plainly that it cannot remove skin. Another clinic notes that using Botox specifically to lift the brow is off-label.
- **Fillers and lasers are still procedures.** Medical News Today lists dermal fillers and laser therapy among treatment options, but they don't remove the fold of skin, and they carry their own risks and costs. The same reasoning applies: they aren't a styling fix we suggest.
- **Results aren't permanent.** A brow lift can last years, but one surgeon's article notes that aging continues afterward.
- **Surgery carries surgical risks.** Any operation around the eyes involves risks such as scarring, asymmetry and complications, as well as recovery time and cost.

The only situation where this becomes relevant is a medical one: if hooding or a drooping lid blocks your vision, or a lid changes quickly, see an eye doctor. They can work out the cause and discuss options for that vision problem. That's a medical decision made with a qualified professional, not a way to change your appearance, and we don't suggest pursuing any of these procedures for looks.

## Putting it together: a realistic plan

If you want a simple path, here's a sensible order, offered as an editorial suggestion:

1. **Do the mirror check** in even light with a relaxed forehead, and take a straight-on reference photo.
2. **Rule out red flags.** Blocked vision, fast change or a lid edge over your pupil means see a doctor first.
3. **Fix your photo setup**: camera at or slightly above eye level, front light, level head.
4. **Groom your brows** from underneath and set them upward.
5. **If you wear makeup,** move shadow above the crease, keep liner close to the lashes, and test thin versus slightly bolder lines with your eyes open.
6. **Re-shoot your reference photo** in the same conditions and compare honestly.

Hooded eyes are common, often inherited, and usually harmless. Knowing which structure creates your hood, and working with it instead of against it, gets you further than any single trick.
