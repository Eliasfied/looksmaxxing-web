You usually notice them in a photo where you were laughing hard: fine lines fanning out from the outer corners of your eyes. Crow's feet are one of the first things people spot when they start studying their own face, and the market around them is loud. Creams promise to erase them, gadgets promise to smooth them, and clinics promise to freeze them.

This guide is more measured. It covers:

- what crow's feet are in anatomical terms,
- why they form and how to tell what kind you have,
- which everyday habits are within your control,
- where the evidence is solid and where it is thin.

It also explains why we don't recommend injectables for appearance, even though they are the most studied option.

## What crow's feet actually are

In clinical writing, crow's feet are called **lateral canthal lines** or **lateral canthal rhytides**. The terms break down simply:

- **Canthus**: the corner where your upper and lower eyelids meet.
- **Lateral**: the outer side, toward your temple.
- **Rhytides**: the medical word for wrinkles.

The lines sit over the **orbicularis oculi**, the ring-shaped muscle around each eye. It closes your eyelids, and it contracts when you squint or smile with your whole face. Every time it tightens, the thin skin at the outer corner folds into small creases. After years of expressions, those creases are where lines show up first.

This is a normal feature of an expressive face. An ET-01 trial protocol registered on ClinicalTrials.gov notes that the periorbital area, meaning the region around the eyes, is one of the first places on the face to show signs of aging. If you notice lines here before anywhere else, that is the usual pattern, not a sign you did something wrong.

### Dynamic vs static lines

The most useful distinction is between two types of wrinkle. A [Loyola University trial record on ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01586819) describes them clearly:

- **Dynamic lines** come from muscle activity and tend to disappear when you relax your face. The record lists crow's feet among the most common dynamic wrinkles, alongside frown lines and forehead lines.
- **Static lines** form when that muscle activity goes on long enough that the creases no longer fully disappear at rest.

If your crow's feet only appear when you smile, they are dynamic. If you can see them with a neutral face, some of them have become static.

### They don't all look the same

Crow's feet also differ in shape. A daxibotulinumtoxinA trial protocol cites work by Carruthers and colleagues describing four patterns: full-fan, lower-fan, central-fan and upper-fan. The names describe where the lines spread from the outer eye corner:

- across the whole area,
- mainly below,
- mainly in the middle,
- mainly above.

You don't need to classify yourself precisely. The point is that "crow's feet" covers a range of looks. That is one reason generic before-and-after photos tell you little about your own face.

## Why crow's feet form

No single cause explains them. The same daxibotulinumtoxinA protocol attributes lines at rest to a combination of factors: repeated facial muscle contractions, structural changes in the skin that come with aging, and damage from excessive sun exposure. Here is how each piece fits.

### Repeated expression

Smiling, laughing and squinting all engage the orbicularis oculi. The movement itself is harmless, and nobody should try to smile less. But repeating it for years is how a dynamic line becomes a static one.

### Changes in skin structure

[Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24586-crows-feet) explains that collagen and elastin naturally decrease as you age. These are the two proteins that keep skin plump and springy, and as they wane, fine lines such as crow's feet develop. With less support, the same fold leaves a more lasting mark.

### Sun and squinting

The trial literature names sun exposure as one contributor to lines at rest. Squinting adds a second effect. Cleveland Clinic points out that sunglasses or a wide-brimmed hat shield your eyes from UV rays and help prevent squinting, which can contribute to crow's feet. Bright light makes you contract the same muscle that creates these lines.

## How to check your own crow's feet

Clinicians don't judge crow's feet from a single look. A comparative study on topical botulinum toxin notes that two severity scales exist: one for wrinkles at rest and one for wrinkles at maximum smile. You can borrow that logic at home without any scoring system.

*Editorial suggestion:* take two photos in the same window light, at the same distance.

1. **Neutral face.** Relax completely and look straight at the camera.
2. **Full smile.** Smile the way you do when something is actually funny.

Then compare them:

- Lines that only show in the smiling photo are dynamic.
- Lines visible in the neutral photo are partly static.

Repeat the same setup every few months if you want to see whether anything is changing. Consistent lighting matters more than camera quality, because harsh overhead light exaggerates every crease.

## Sun protection: the part you control most

Of everything in this article, daily sun protection has the clearest backing from a medical source. Cleveland Clinic recommends sunscreen with **SPF 30 or higher**. It also suggests **sunglasses or a wide-brimmed hat** to protect the eye area and reduce squinting.

Editorial suggestions for making that stick:

- **Carry sunscreen out to the outer eye corner.** Many people stop at the cheekbone and skip the area where crow's feet form. Apply carefully and keep it out of your eyes.
- **Choose sunglasses with UV protection,** not just dark lenses. Larger frames or a wraparound shape cover more of the outer corner.
- **Keep a hat in your bag or car** for long stretches outdoors, when sunglasses alone leave your temples exposed.
- **Notice other squinting.** If you catch yourself squinting at screens or distant signs, book an eye exam for its own sake.

None of this erases lines that are already there. The goal is to avoid adding sun damage and squinting on top of normal aging.

## Skincare: realistic expectations

The skincare aisle is where claims about crow's feet get most inflated. Here is what the evidence we found actually says.

- **Topical acids and retinoids.** An ET-01 trial protocol lists topical agents such as retinoic acids, glycolic acids and lactic acids as common methods used to minimize crow's feet. That is a background statement about what people use, not a measured result. We did not find efficacy data strong enough to report here.
- **Prescription retinoic acid** is a prescription drug. Whether it suits your skin is a decision for a doctor, and this site does not recommend prescription drugs for appearance.
- **Moisturizers and eye creams.** *Editorial view:* a moisturizer keeps the skin around your eyes comfortable and less dry, which is a fine reason to use one. We found no solid evidence that any specific eye cream removes crow's feet. Treat "erase" and "instant lift" language with skepticism.

If you try a new active product, introduce one at a time and keep it off the eyelid itself. The skin there is thin and reacts easily.

## Botox, peels and procedures: our position

Botulinum toxin is the most studied treatment for crow's feet, and pretending otherwise would be misleading. One study was a [13-month, phase 3, double-blind, randomized trial in Japanese subjects](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5605642/). It notes that onabotulinumtoxinA is approved for crow's feet lines in more than 50 countries, including the USA and EU countries. Researchers have also studied medium-depth chemical peels for these lines.

**We don't recommend Botox, other injectables, or medical peels for appearance.** The main downsides are concrete:

- **Botox is a prescription neurotoxin injected near the eye.** It works by limiting the muscle you use to smile with your eyes.
- **The effect is temporary.** The Japanese trial allowed up to five treatments over 13 months, based on retreatment criteria. Choosing it means choosing repeat injections.
- **Not everyone is a candidate.** One ET-01 trial excluded people with neuromuscular disease, eyelid ptosis (a drooping upper lid), muscle weakness, dry eye, or a history of eye-area surgery. It also excluded people who were pregnant or nursing. These exclusions exist because the eye area is a sensitive place to intervene.
- **Medium-depth peels** are a medical procedure with their own recovery period and risks.

> **Talk to a qualified professional before considering this.** If you have a medical concern about your eyes or skin, raise it with a doctor or dermatologist, not a blog post or an app.

The non-medical options in this guide come first: sun protection, sleep, grooming and styling. They are lower risk and within your control.

## Styling and grooming around the eye area

How crow's feet read in daily life depends heavily on what surrounds them. These are *editorial suggestions*, not measured effects.

### Frames and eyewear

Glasses change where people look. A frame with a defined upper rim draws attention to the brow line and the eye itself. Thin or rimless frames put more focus on the skin at the corners. If you wear glasses, treat frame shape as part of how your eye area looks, not only as a prescription decision.

### Makeup, if you wear it

- Use a thin layer of creamy concealer or a tinted product at the outer corner rather than building coverage.
- Go light with loose powder there, since powder tends to sit in creases and make lines more visible.
- Blend outward with a fingertip or damp sponge, and check the result in daylight, not bathroom light.

### Grooming and rest

Well-shaped brows, a current haircut and, if you have facial hair, a neat beard line all frame the eyes. Short sleep tends to make the whole eye area look tired, which draws attention to lines that would otherwise go unnoticed. None of these change the lines themselves. They change the overall impression.

## A simple daily routine for the eye area

*Editorial suggestion*, built around the habits above:

1. **Morning:** cleanse gently, moisturize, then apply SPF 30 or higher all the way to the outer eye corners.
2. **Before going out:** wear sunglasses with UV protection, plus a hat for long periods in direct sun.
3. **During the day:** notice when you squint at a screen or into light, and adjust brightness, distance or position.
4. **Evening:** remove sunscreen and makeup gently, without dragging the skin, then moisturize.
5. **Every few months:** retake your neutral and smiling photos in the same light to track the area calmly.

This routine will not make crow's feet disappear. It is a sensible baseline that addresses the sun and squinting factors you control.

## Keeping crow's feet in perspective

Crow's feet come from the same muscle movement that makes a smile look genuine. Lines that appear when you laugh are a sign of an expressive face. Protect your skin from the sun, groom and style the area with intention, and be skeptical of anyone promising to erase a normal part of having a face that moves.
