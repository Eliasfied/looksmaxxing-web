Look at any side profile and your eye follows a path from the forehead down into the nose. The point where that line bends is the **nasofrontal angle**. Rhinoplasty surgeons use it in profile analysis, and looksmaxxing forums argue about it.

The numbers attached to it are easy to misread. A textbook "ideal" range is not the same as what real people measure, and neither tells you how attractive a face is. This guide covers what the angle is, where the commonly quoted numbers come from, how to measure it without fooling yourself, and what you can realistically do with that information.

## What the nasofrontal angle actually is

The nasofrontal angle is measured on a **side profile**. It uses three landmarks:

- **Glabella**: the smooth, slightly raised area of the forehead between the eyebrows.
- **Nasion**: the deepest point where the forehead dips into the bridge of the nose. Surgeons also call this area the **radix**.
- **Nasal tip**: the most forward point of the nose.

Textbook rhinoplasty references define the angle with two lines. One runs from the glabella down to the nasion. The other runs from the nasion out to the nasal tip. The angle between them, opening at the nasion, is the nasofrontal angle.

In plain terms, it describes **how sharply your forehead turns into your nose**. Surgeons often call this transition the *nasal starting point*, because it marks where the nose visually begins.

### Deep versus shallow

Two words come up constantly:

- A **deeper, more acute** angle (a smaller number) means a clear dip between the brow and the nasal bridge.
- A **shallower, more obtuse** angle (a larger number) means the forehead flows into the nose with little dip. At the extreme it approaches a straight line, sometimes described as a "Greek" profile.

Neither is a defect. They are different shapes, and both appear in faces widely considered attractive.

## The "ideal" range and where it comes from

The most frequently quoted range is roughly **115 to 130 degrees**. A clinical paper on facial angles for rhinoplasty, published on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9758669/), traces this range to Powell and Humphreys' concept of facial aesthetic triangles. That framework remains a standard reference in surgical profile analysis.

The sources don't fully agree on the upper limit. Several textbook-style chapters on nasal analysis use **115 to 135 degrees** instead. So the honest summary is "about 115 to 130 degrees, with some references allowing up to 135."

### Differences between men and women

Rhinoplasty references generally state that, *within* the normal range:

- A **more obtuse** (shallower) angle is considered more favorable in women.
- A **more acute** (deeper) angle is considered more favorable in men.

A prospective study on primary rhinoplasty in Caucasian patients describes this as a consensus position. It ties the preference to greater tip rotation in women. Treat this as a general aesthetic convention, not a rule. Plenty of women have deep nasal starting points and plenty of men have shallow ones, without anything looking "off."

## Ideal vs. measured vs. preferred: three different numbers

This is where most online discussion goes wrong. People see "115 to 130 degrees," measure their own photo, get 138, and assume something is wrong. Look at what studies actually find.

| Source | What was measured | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Powell and Humphreys framework | Textbook aesthetic ideal | About 115 to 130° |
| Turkish photographic study | Average angle in real adults | About 138.5° in men, 141.6° in women (digital measurement) |
| Indian clinical study (30 people) | Average angle in a small sample | 116.7° overall, ranging from 92.8° to 140° |
| Thai preference study (1,452 people) | Angle people *chose* as ideal | Roughly 126 to 127° |

A few takeaways:

1. **Real averages can sit outside the "ideal."** The [Turkish anthropometry study](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3745343) found mean values above 135 degrees for both sexes. In that sample, women again measured more obtuse than men. If your number lands above 130, you are not an outlier compared with real faces.
2. **Spread is wide.** The small Indian sample ran from under 93 to 140 degrees. Normal human variation covers far more than a 15-degree window.
3. **Preference depends on who you ask.** A large [study of nasal aesthetics in Thailand](https://doi.org/10.1177/20503121241299066) compared people considering rhinoplasty with a control group. Both groups preferred angles around 126 to 127 degrees. Japanese researchers studying nasal angle preferences start from the premise that ideal nasal shape differs across nationalities and cultures.

So the "ideal range" is a surgical planning reference. It is not a pass or fail line for your face.

## How to measure your nasofrontal angle (without fooling yourself)

You can estimate the angle from a profile photo. Small setup errors shift the result by several degrees, though, so consistency matters more than precision.

### Step-by-step

1. **Use a true side profile.** Stand sideways to the camera so only one eye is visible. A three-quarter view distorts the angle.
2. **Keep the camera at eye level.** Use a tripod or a stable surface. Shooting from below or above changes how the brow and nose line up.
3. **Level your head.** Look straight ahead at a fixed point, as if looking at the horizon. Tilting the chin up or down changes the measurement.
4. **Step back and zoom slightly.** Holding a phone close to the face exaggerates the nose with wide-angle distortion. A bit of distance gives a more natural shape.
5. **Pull hair off the forehead.** You need a clear view of the glabella.
6. **Mark the three points.** In any image editor or angle-measuring app, place points at the glabella, the nasion (deepest point of the dip) and the nasal tip.
7. **Draw the two lines and read the angle** at the nasion.
8. **Repeat three times** with fresh photos and average the results.

### Why your number will wobble

- **Landmark choice is subjective.** Where exactly is the "most prominent" point of the glabella? Two people marking the same photo can disagree.
- **Soft tissue matters.** Eyebrow thickness, brow bone padding and skin all sit on top of the bone.
- **Lens and posture effects are real.** A few degrees of head tilt can move the result noticeably.

A self-measured number is a rough estimate. It is not a diagnosis, and it shouldn't drive major decisions.

## How the angle shapes the look of your nose

The nasofrontal angle doesn't just describe the top of the nose. It changes how the **whole nose reads**, including from the front.

- **A very obtuse angle can make the nose look longer.** Rhinoplasty references note that with a shallow transition there is no clear starting point. The eye reads the nose as beginning higher up the face.
- **A small, sharp angle can make the nose look shorter.** Surgeons' nasal analysis guides describe the reverse effect: a deep dip sets a low starting point.
- **The radix height matters too.** Where the dip sits vertically, relative to the eyes, changes the perceived length of the nose.

This is why two noses with the same length in millimeters can look quite different on two faces.

### Related angles you'll see mentioned

The nasofrontal angle is usually read alongside two others:

- **Nasolabial angle**: the angle between the columella (the strip of skin between the nostrils) and the upper lip. It reflects tip rotation. Common references give a normal range of about 90 to 120 degrees.
- **Nasofacial angle**: how far the nose projects from the plane of the face, typically cited at 30 to 40 degrees.

No single angle defines a good profile. Surgeons look at how these angles relate to the forehead, chin, lips and overall face, not at one number in isolation.

## What you can and can't change without surgery

Your bony nasofrontal angle is set by your skull and nasal bones. Grooming won't change the measurement itself. You can still change **how that area reads** in everyday life and in photos. The suggestions below are editorial styling advice, not measured effects.

### Brows

- Your brows frame the glabella and the start of the nose. Well-shaped, tidy brows give the area clear definition.
- Over-plucking the inner brows can make the space between the eyes look wider and flatter. Over-grown inner brows can visually crowd the nasion.

### Glasses

- Glasses sit right on the radix, so the bridge design matters.
- If your nasal starting point is **low or shallow**, a frame with a higher bridge or adjustable nose pads can sit more naturally.
- If your dip is **deep**, a keyhole or lower bridge often sits comfortably without sliding.

### Makeup (if you wear it)

- Many people with a shallow transition use soft contour at the inner brow corners to suggest more depth. Others with a deep dip prefer a subtle highlight down the bridge. Both are preferences, not corrections.

### Hair, beard and profile balance

- Bangs and a fringe change how much of the forehead and glabella shows in profile.
- For men, a beard that adds definition to the chin and jawline changes overall profile balance. The nose becomes one feature among several rather than the focal point.

### Photos

- Most profile photos that feel unflattering come from close, low-angle shots. Shooting from slightly further away at eye level is the simplest fix available.

## A note on rhinoplasty

The angle can change with surgery. A prospective study of primary rhinoplasty found significant differences between preoperative and postoperative nasofrontal angles. On average the angle increased in men and decreased in women, the latter linked to lowering the nasal dorsum, such as removing a hump.

**We don't recommend rhinoplasty for appearance.** It is major facial surgery. Its main risks include:

- Anesthesia complications, bleeding and infection.
- Swelling that can take many months to fully settle.
- Changes to nasal breathing.
- Results that don't match what you pictured.
- The possibility of revision surgery.

An angle outside a textbook range is not a reason for surgery. As the studies above show, many healthy, attractive faces measure outside it. Start with the non-medical options: grooming, glasses choice, hairstyle and how you're photographed. If you have a genuine functional problem, such as difficulty breathing through your nose, that is a medical question. **Talk to a qualified professional before considering this.**

## Practical takeaways

- **Treat 115 to 130 degrees as a reference, not a verdict.** Real averages often run higher, and preferences vary by culture.
- **Measure consistently or not at all.** Same distance, eye-level camera, level head, three photos averaged.
- **Read the angle in context.** It interacts with brow position, radix height, tip rotation and chin projection.
- **Focus on what you control.** Brows, frames, hair, beard and photo setup change how your profile reads far more than a few degrees on paper.
- **Be skeptical of single-number ratings.** Any tool or forum that judges your face on one angle is oversimplifying.
