Jaw implants come up constantly in looksmaxxing discussions as the "permanent fix" for a narrow or soft jawline. They are a real procedure with a real effect. They are also cosmetic surgery: a foreign body placed on the jawbone, with risks that can mean more surgery later.

This guide covers what jaw implants are, how the surgery works, what can go wrong, and what to try first.

## What Are Jaw Implants?

Jaw implants are solid prosthetics placed over the lower jaw (the mandible) to change its shape, width or projection. They are distinct from chin implants, which target only the front-most point of the jaw. Jaw implants typically address the **mandibular angle** (the back corners of the jaw), the body of the mandible, or both.

### Types of Jaw Implants

- **Silicone implants:** The most widely used option. Solid silicone is firm but slightly flexible. It does not integrate with bone, which means it can be removed.
- **Porous polyethylene (Medpor):** A stiffer material that tissue and bone grow into over time. That makes it more stable, and much harder to remove or revise.
- **Custom implants:** Designed from a CT scan of the skull and made to match the patient's anatomy. They fit better than standard sizes, but they are still implants with the same categories of risk.

## The Risks and Complications

Every surgical procedure carries risk. These are the ones that matter most for jaw implants.

### Common

- **Numbness and nerve damage.** The mental nerve runs along the jaw and supplies sensation to the chin and lower lip. It can be stretched, compressed or injured during surgery. Temporary numbness is common. Permanent nerve damage is less common, but it happens.
- **Asymmetry.** Even with careful planning, the two sides can end up different. Correcting that means another operation.
- **Swelling and pain.** Significant for the first weeks, with limited mouth opening and a soft diet.

### Serious

- **Infection.** A particular concern with incisions inside the mouth. A serious infection can mean the implant has to come out.
- **Implant migration.** An implant that is not secured well, or that is knocked, can shift and change the result.
- **Bone resorption.** Long-term pressure from an implant can thin the bone underneath it.
- **Capsular contracture.** Scar tissue forming around the implant can harden and distort its shape.

### Revision

Revision surgery after jaw implants is not rare. Implants get repositioned, exchanged or removed because of shifting, infection, asymmetry, or because the patient is unhappy with the result. Each revision is another operation, and removal does not always restore how the face looked before.

## If Your Goal Is a Sharper Jaw: Start Without Surgery

Nothing non-surgical adds bone. But a large share of how defined a jaw looks has nothing to do with bone:

- **Body fat.** Fat under the chin and along the jaw hides the jawline that is already there. A few percent less body fat often changes jaw definition more than people expect.
- **Posture.** A head-forward posture blurs the line between jaw and neck. Standing and sitting upright makes the jaw angle visible again.
- **Facial hair.** A well-shaped beard can add visual width and a sharper line along the jaw. Even a cleaner neckline changes how the jaw reads.
- **Hairstyle.** A haircut that suits your face shape changes the overall balance of the face, including how wide the jaw looks.

These changes are slower and smaller than surgery. They are also reversible and carry no surgical risk, which is why they come first.

Two things often presented as "non-surgical alternatives" are still medical procedures: **dermal fillers** along the jaw carry vascular risks and should only be considered with an experienced medical injector, and **fat-dissolving injections** have their own side effects. ## The Surgical Procedure: What Actually Happens

Jaw implant surgery is almost always performed under general anesthesia.

### Incision Placement

The most common approach uses incisions **inside the mouth**, along the lower gum line near the back molars, which leaves no visible external scar. For angle implants, a small external incision is sometimes made under the jawline.

### Implant Placement

The surgeon creates a pocket under the periosteum (the dense tissue covering the bone) and places the implant against the bone. It is usually secured with small titanium screws to prevent migration. The procedure typically takes one to two hours.

### Combined Procedures

Jaw implants are often combined with chin implants, rhinoplasty or buccal fat removal. Every added procedure adds its own risks and extends the recovery.

## Recovery Timeline

Recovery from jaw implants is longer and more uncomfortable than many patients anticipate:

**Days 1 to 3:** Significant swelling, pain managed with prescribed medication, limited mouth opening. A liquid or very soft diet is necessary.

**Week 1 to 2:** Swelling peaks around day three to five, then begins to reduce. The jaw feels tight and movement is restricted.

**Weeks 3 to 6:** Most visible swelling resolves. Strenuous activity is still restricted to reduce the risk of the implant shifting.

**Months 3 to 6:** The implant settles into position and the result can be judged.

**Up to a year:** Some patients report that firmness and minor swelling persist, particularly with porous polyethylene implants.

Because the incisions are inside the mouth, careful oral hygiene and prescribed antiseptic rinses and antibiotics are part of reducing infection risk.

## Cost

Jaw implant surgery is almost never covered by insurance unless it is part of reconstruction after trauma or a documented medical condition. Rough ranges:

- **Standard silicone implants:** $5,000 to $10,000 total (surgeon fee, anesthesia, facility)
- **Custom implants:** $10,000 to $20,000 or more, depending on design and scanning

Revisions, removal and treatment of complications are usually extra.

## If You Are Still Considering It

If you have tried the non-surgical route and are still weighing implants, ask any surgeon these questions before agreeing to anything:

1. **What is your own rate of nerve damage, infection and revision?** Their numbers, not general literature figures.
2. **What happens if I want the implant removed?** Ask what the face is likely to look like afterwards and what removal costs.
3. **Which material, and why?** Porous materials are harder to remove. Make sure you understand what you are choosing.
4. **What do patients with my starting point look like years later?** Not just the best results in the portfolio.
5. **What is your revision policy?** Who pays when something has to be corrected.

A surgeon who brushes these questions aside, or who pushes you toward combining procedures, is not the right person to operate on your face.
