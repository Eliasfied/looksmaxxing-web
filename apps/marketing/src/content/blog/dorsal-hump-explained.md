You catch your side profile in a photo and notice a bump on the bridge of your nose. A search later, you have a name for it: **dorsal hump**. It is one of the most common nose features there is, and it gets a lot of attention online.

This guide covers what a dorsal hump is, how clinicians describe the different types, and what usually causes one. It also explains how a hump shows up in photos and what you can change without a medical procedure. Finally, it explains why we don't recommend surgery or fillers for appearance, and what the main risks are.

## What a dorsal hump actually is

A dorsal hump is a raised area of **bone, cartilage, or both** on the bridge of the nose. It is not a growth, a swelling or a condition. It is the shape of the nose's own structure.

It is also very common. A peer-reviewed paper on [hump nose and nasal deviation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12786902/) describes dorsal humps and deviation as common deformities in rhinoplasty patients. In everyday life, plenty of people with a visible bump never think about it. A bump on the bridge is normal anatomy.

### Why it's called "dorsal"

*Dorsum* is the anatomical word for the back or top surface of a structure. On the nose, the **nasal dorsum** is the bridge. It runs from the area between your eyes down toward the tip. So "dorsal hump" just means a hump on the bridge.

### Bone, cartilage, or both

The bridge is made of two materials:

- **The bony vault.** The paired nasal bones form the upper part of the bridge.
- **The cartilaginous vault.** Cartilage supports the lower part of the bridge. This is mainly the septum in the middle and the upper lateral cartilages on each side.

The point where bone meets cartilage is called the **rhinion**. Many humps sit around this junction. That is why they often look like a ridge partway down the bridge rather than at the top or near the tip.

## The types of dorsal hump

Surgeons classify humps mainly to plan operations. The categories still help you understand your own profile. Traditional systems sort humps by their surface outline into three broad groups:

1. **Isolated hump.** The paper defines this as a focal bump centred at the rhinion that interrupts an otherwise smooth bridge. This is the classic "bump in the middle" profile.
2. **Generalized hump.** This is a broad, gentle curve that spans both the bony and cartilaginous parts of the bridge. It reads more as an overall convex line than as a single bump.
3. **Relative hump.** This is a hump that looks more prominent because of how the surrounding parts of the nose sit.

Newer classifications from preservation rhinoplasty add more detail. They consider the shape of the nasal bones, what the hump is made of, and how much reduction a surgeon would expect to make.

**The practical takeaway:** how big a hump *looks* depends partly on its context. That includes the rest of the nose, the rest of the face, lighting and camera angle. This is why styling can change how a hump reads, even though the bone and cartilage stay the same.

## What causes a dorsal hump

There is no precise breakdown of causes. Clinical and clinic sources agree on the general picture:

- **Genetics.** This is the most common cause. Nose shape is largely inherited, so if a parent or grandparent has a bump on the bridge, that is usually the explanation.
- **Injury.** A knock to the nose, especially during growth, can change how the bone and cartilage develop or heal.
- **Uneven healing or structural imbalance.** A UK clinic's condition page lists these as further causes alongside genetics and injury.

A dorsal hump is structural. Because it is formed by bone and cartilage, nose exercises, massage, taping, skincare or diet won't change it. There is no support for claims that they do.

## Does a dorsal hump affect breathing?

On its own, usually not. The hump sits on the outer surface of the bridge, above the airway. For most people, the concern is about appearance.

Breathing problems are a separate issue. Signs include constant one-sided blockage, heavy snoring, or a nose that feels obstructed after an old injury. These can come from things like a deviated septum and need a doctor, ideally an ENT (ear, nose and throat) specialist. Don't assume a visible bump is the cause, and don't assume it isn't.

## How a dorsal hump reads on your face

People usually notice their dorsal hump in three situations:

- **Side-profile photos**, where the bridge line is fully visible.
- **Close-up phone selfies.** The wide-angle lens exaggerates whatever is closest to the camera, which is the nose.
- **Harsh overhead or side light.** It casts a shadow just below the hump and catches a highlight on top of it.

Face-on in normal conversation, most humps are barely visible. A hump stands out most in profile. Profile is also where the rest of your styling has the most influence.

## Non-medical ways to change how a dorsal hump reads

The ideas below are **editorial styling suggestions**, not measured effects. None of them change the nose itself. They change balance, light and attention, and those shape how a feature reads in practice.

### Hair

- **Add volume at the crown or back of the head.** In profile, a flat head shape leaves the nose as the most projected point. Some height or fullness behind it rebalances the silhouette.
- **Avoid very severe, flat, slicked-back styles** if your hump bothers you in profile. Texture and movement give the eye more than one focal point.
- **For longer hair**, try soft face-framing layers, a side part or curtain bangs. They break up the straight vertical line from forehead to tip. Choose what fits your own style. There is no single "correct" look.

### Beard and jawline grooming

If you wear facial hair, a defined outline along the jaw and chin strengthens the lower third of the face in profile. That gives the nose a counterweight. Keep the neckline clean and avoid letting the chin area look patchy or undefined.

### Glasses

If you wear glasses, the frame's bridge sits right where many humps are, so frame choice matters more than people expect.

- Frames that rest **at or slightly above the hump** tend to break up the bridge line rather than outline it.
- A **keyhole bridge** or a frame with a stronger top rim pulls attention horizontally across the eyes.
- Very thin, low-set metal frames can sit awkwardly on a prominent bridge and draw attention to it.

### Makeup, if you wear it

- Apply **matte** contour lightly down the sides of the nose, not over the hump.
- Keep **highlighter off the bump**. Shine on the most projected point makes it look more projected. If you highlight, use a small amount on the smooth part of the bridge or skip the nose.
- Blend well. Visible contour stripes stand out more in profile than the hump does.

### Photos, light and posture

- **Step back and zoom in.** Using your phone's 2x lens from further away reduces the wide-angle distortion that enlarges the nose in close selfies.
- **Use soft light from the front**, such as a window you are facing. Hard side light carves a shadow under the hump.
- **Choose your angle.** Straight-on or a slight three-quarter turn shows the bridge less than a full profile does.
- **Keep your chin level and your head stacked over your shoulders.** A forward-jutting head pushes the nose further out in profile shots.

### Overall face balance

General habits shape how the whole face reads in profile. These include consistent sleep, a healthy body-fat level that keeps the jaw and chin defined, and good posture. They don't touch the nose, but they change what surrounds it.

## Rhinoplasty and nasal fillers: our position

These procedures come up in almost every dorsal hump search, so here is a direct answer. **We don't recommend rhinoplasty or nasal fillers for appearance.** Try the non-medical options above first. **Talk to a qualified professional before considering this.**

### Main risks of rhinoplasty

- **Serious complications can happen.** A medical case report describes [dorsal skin necrosis after rhinoplasty](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12578537/) in patients treated with topical retinoids. Necrosis means loss of skin over the bridge. It is a reminder that surgery on the nose carries real risks.
- **Outcomes differ between techniques.** A [meta-analysis comparing dorsal hump reduction techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39185377/) found patient-reported outcomes broadly comparable. It also suggested that one approach may relieve obstructive symptoms better than another, so the choice of technique matters.
- **Breathing can be affected.** A hospital group's patient guide notes that poorly supported hump removal can narrow the middle of the nose over time. The surgery itself can create a breathing problem that the hump never caused.
- **The evidence comparing techniques is still developing.** Studies comparing newer "preservation" approaches with conventional hump removal do not all agree. No technique comes without trade-offs.

A genuine breathing problem, such as an obstructed airway after an injury, is a separate question from appearance. Whether to treat it is a decision to make with a doctor.

### Main limits and risks of nasal fillers

Fillers do not remove a dorsal hump. Clinic sources describe filler injected around the bump to camouflage it, and the result is temporary. Injecting into the nose is a medical procedure with its own risks, which a qualified doctor would need to explain. We don't recommend it for appearance.

## Other meanings of "dorsal hump"

Occasionally the phrase refers to animals rather than noses. A bison has a hump between its shoulder blades, and unlike a camel's, it is formed from muscle. If you came here for zoology, that is the short answer. The rest of this guide is about the nose.

## The bottom line

A dorsal hump is a common, structural feature of the nasal bridge. It is made of bone, cartilage or both, and is usually inherited. It rarely affects breathing on its own. How it reads depends on context: lighting, lens, angle, hair, grooming, glasses and overall facial balance. Those are the levers you can control safely. Work on them first, and treat any breathing concern as a separate conversation with a doctor.
