You noticed your temples looked a little higher in a bathroom photo, searched for answers, and now every forum thread is throwing "NW2" and "NW3" around like a shared language. That shorthand is the Norwood scale, the most common way to describe male pattern hair loss.

This guide walks through every stage in plain terms. It covers the variant most people miss, and the slang that isn't part of the real scale. It also explains what the scale can and can't tell you about your own hairline. The goal is a clear head, not panic.

## What the Norwood Scale Actually Is

The Norwood scale is a visual classification system for **androgenetic alopecia**, the medical name for male pattern hair loss. In this condition, hair follicles go through *miniaturisation*. Each growth cycle produces a finer, shorter hair until some follicles only make faint fuzz.

### Where it came from

The scale has two authors, which is why you will see it called the Hamilton-Norwood or Norwood-Hamilton scale.

- **James Hamilton** published the first classification of male pattern baldness in 1951.
- **O'Tar Norwood** studied 1,000 Caucasian men in 1975. He simplified Hamilton's system and removed a confusing catch-all category, producing the version still in use.

That 1975 revision is still the reference point in clinical research. Trial protocols for hair loss studies reproduce Norwood's chart and cite "Norwood 1975" as the source.

### What it is designed to describe

The scale was built for one mechanism only: androgenetic alopecia. It describes the **shape and extent** of pattern hair loss. It does not tell you *why* you are losing hair. Patchy loss, sudden shedding or loss with scalp irritation can have entirely different causes, and the scale was never meant to sort those out.

Pattern hair loss is extremely common. A meta-analysis in the dermatology literature reports that [androgenetic alopecia affects 50 to 60% of men by age 50 and 80% by age 70](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12537375/). If you are seeing early signs, you are in large company.

## The 7 Norwood Stages, One by One

The main scale has **seven primary patterns**, from Type 1 (minimal loss) to Type 7 (most severe). Here is what each looks like.

### Stage 1: No real recession

In the clinical definition used in trials, Stage 1 means [no recession or very minimal recession along the front hairline at the temples](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/73/NCT05802173/SAP_001.pdf). The term for that area is the *frontotemporal* region.

### Stage 2: Small, symmetrical triangles

The hairline at the temples develops **triangular areas of recession** that tend to be symmetrical. The middle of the hairline may also move back slightly, but much less than the temples. Many men sit at this stage for a long time, and it is not classed as baldness in Norwood's framework.

### Stage 3: Where "balding" officially begins

This is the key threshold. Norwood's definition describes Type 3 as the **minimal extent of hair loss considered sufficient to represent baldness**. The temple recessions are deep, usually symmetrical, and bare or very sparsely covered.

Seen from above, the hairline often forms a clear "M" shape. Hair loss generally becomes noticeable to other people here.

### Stage 3 Vertex: Crown first

Some men keep a Stage 3 style front hairline but start thinning at the **vertex**, the crown at the back of the top of the head. Norwood split this out as its own pattern because the crown can thin before the front changes much.

### Stages 4 to 6: Front and crown converge

- **Stage 4:** The front recession is deeper and the crown has a defined thinning area. A band of hair still separates the two zones.
- **Stage 5:** That band narrows and thins, so the front and crown areas start to look like one region.
- **Stage 6:** The band is gone. The front and crown have joined into one larger area of loss across the top.

### Stage 7: The horseshoe

Stage 7 is the most advanced stage on the original scale. Only a **narrow, horseshoe-shaped rim of hair** remains at the sides and the base of the scalp.

## The Type A Variant Most People Miss

Alongside the standard progression, Norwood described a separate family of patterns called **Type A**. In these cases, the hairline recedes from front to back as a whole line. It does not form deep temple triangles and a separate bald spot on the crown.

A few points about Type A:

- It affects a **small minority** of men. One detailed guide puts it at well under 10% of cases in Norwood's data. Because it is uncommon, it is easy to misread as a standard stage.
- The accurate labels are **Norwood II A, III A, IV A and V A**. They are variants of specific stages, not independent stages of their own.
- If your hairline is moving back evenly without obvious temple points, you may be looking at a Type A pattern rather than a "weird" standard one.

## Norwood 0, Norwood 8 and Other Slang

Online communities have stretched the scale beyond its original design. It helps to know which labels are real.

- **"Norwood 0":** Informal. It is used for an unusually low, dense, often straight juvenile hairline. Norwood's scale starts at 1.
- **"Norwood 8":** Also informal. Online, it describes complete or near-complete baldness, sometimes with thinning on the sides and back. Norwood's original scale ends at Stage 7, so on the official scale these cases fall under Stage 7.
- **"NW2", "NW3" and similar:** Simple shorthand for the real stages. These are fine to use, as long as everyone remembers they are rough visual labels.

Community labels like these document how people talk. They are not medical categories.

## What the Norwood Scale Can't Tell You

The scale is useful as a shared vocabulary, but it has real limits.

### It is subjective

Doctors use the scale regularly, but [it is not considered very reliable, since different examiners can reach different conclusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton%E2%80%93Norwood_scale) about the same scalp. Lighting, hair length, styling and camera angle all change how a hairline reads. If two experienced clinicians can disagree, a single selfie is weak evidence.

### It describes, it doesn't forecast

Some sources say the scale can help identify patterns that may predict progression. In practice, treat it as a **snapshot**. Being at Stage 2 today tells you where you are, not how fast you will move or whether you will move at all. Progression varies a lot between people.

### It doesn't diagnose a cause

The scale assumes pattern hair loss. It cannot rule out other causes, such as patchy loss or sudden diffuse shedding. Those need a proper medical assessment.

### It is built for men

The Norwood scale describes male patterns. Female pattern hair loss usually looks different, with thinning across the top rather than temple recession. It is classified with separate systems such as the **Ludwig-Savin scale**. A woman trying to place herself on a Norwood chart will usually get a misleading answer.

## How to Check Your Own Hairline Honestly

These are editorial suggestions for consistent self-tracking, not a diagnostic method.

1. **Fix your setup.** Use the same spot, the same light source and the same distance every time. Soft daylight from a window beats overhead bathroom bulbs, which exaggerate thinning.
2. **Take three angles.** Shoot the front with your hair pushed back, each temple from the side, and the crown from above. Use a second mirror or ask someone for help with the crown.
3. **Keep hair dry and unstyled.** Wet hair clumps and shows more scalp. Product can hide or exaggerate gaps.
4. **Compare over months, not days.** Day-to-day changes are mostly lighting and styling. Monthly photos side by side give a far more honest picture.
5. **Don't stage yourself from one bad photo.** If a picture looks alarming, retake it in your standard setup before drawing any conclusion.
6. **Note anything that doesn't fit the pattern.** Round bald patches, sudden heavy shedding, itching, scaling or soreness are not what the Norwood scale describes. Those are a reason to see a dermatologist.

## Practical, Non-Medical Ways to Work With Your Hairline

Whatever stage you land on, the levers you control day to day are grooming and styling. These are editorial styling suggestions based on common barber practice, not measured outcomes.

### Haircuts that suit receding temples

- **Shorter sides, a bit of texture on top.** Shorter sides reduce the contrast between the temples and the rest of the head. A textured crop or short fringe pushed slightly forward softens an "M" shape.
- **Avoid slicked-back styles if the temples bother you.** Pulling everything straight back puts the recession on full display.
- **Avoid long hair grown to cover thinning.** Longer hair separates and shows scalp more easily, especially at the crown.

### The buzz cut and the shaved head

At Stage 4 and beyond, many men find a **close buzz or a fully shaved head** looks sharper than fighting the pattern. With very short hair, the difference between dense and thinning areas becomes far less visible. A neat beard or defined stubble can add balance to the face when hair on top is very short.

### Bring a clear brief to your barber

Tell your barber what bothers you, such as the temples, the crown or overall density, rather than naming a stage. A good barber will adjust the length on the sides, the texture on top and the fringe direction to suit your pattern.

### Basics that affect how hair looks

Good sleep, a reasonable body fat level, upright posture and a consistent skincare routine all affect how your face and hair read in photos and in person. None of these changes your Norwood stage. They do change the overall impression, which is what most people actually care about.

## A Note on Hair Loss Medications and Surgery

The Norwood scale is used in clinical trials of hair loss drugs, so you will see minoxidil and finasteride mentioned constantly alongside it. **We don't recommend minoxidil, finasteride, any other prescription drug or hair transplant surgery as a way to change your appearance.** These are not neutral grooming upgrades, and they carry real risks:

- **Finasteride** acts on hormones. Its best-known risks are sexual side effects, such as lower libido and erectile problems, and some people also report mood changes.
- **Minoxidil** can irritate the scalp in topical form. Oral minoxidil is a blood pressure medicine and can affect heart rate and blood pressure, as well as cause unwanted hair growth elsewhere on the body.
- **Both are long-term commitments.** Any effect generally depends on continuing to take them.
- **Hair transplant surgery** is an invasive procedure with surgical risks, cost and recovery time, and it does not stop ongoing pattern loss.

If you notice anything that doesn't fit the usual pattern, such as round patches, sudden heavy shedding, itching or soreness, see a dermatologist so they can examine your scalp and check for other causes. That visit is about your health, not a step toward a drug or procedure for looks.

For appearance, the styling and grooming options above are the low-risk place to start and stay.

## Key Takeaways

- The Norwood scale has **seven main stages**, plus Type A variants (II A to V A) and a Stage 3 Vertex pattern.
- **Stage 3 is the clinical threshold** where Norwood's definition first counts the loss as baldness. Stage 2 recession is common and not classed as balding.
- **Norwood 0 and Norwood 8 are slang**, not official stages.
- The scale is **subjective**, describes rather than predicts, and is built for male pattern loss only.
- Track with **consistent photos over months**. Use styling and grooming to work with your pattern, and see a dermatologist for anything that doesn't fit the usual pattern.
