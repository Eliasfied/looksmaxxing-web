Turn sideways in a mirror and the chin is one of the first things you notice. It anchors the lower third of your face, sets the line from your lips to your neck, and gets discussed online as if there were one correct position. There isn't, quite. *Chin projection* is a real clinical concept with real measurements behind it. The so-called ideal still depends on who is measuring, which method they use, and whose face is being judged.

This guide covers what the term means, how professionals assess it, and what the studies actually show about preferences. It also covers what you can realistically do about how your profile reads, without treating your face as a problem to fix.

## What Chin Projection Actually Means

Chin projection describes how far forward your chin sits when your face is seen in profile. It is almost never judged on its own. Clinicians compare the chin to nearby landmarks, most often the lower lip. Sometimes they use reference lines that run from the forehead or the base of the nose.

A chin that sits well behind those reference points is described as *retruded* or *underprojected*. One that sits well in front is *overprojected*. Most people fall somewhere in a broad middle range.

### Terms Worth Knowing

- **Pogonion:** the most forward point of the chin. *Soft tissue pogonion* means that point on the skin rather than on the bone underneath.
- **Microgenia:** a small chin, where the bony chin itself is underdeveloped.
- **Retrognathia:** the whole lower jaw sits further back. This is about jaw position, not just the chin, and it can involve how your teeth meet.
- **Prognathia:** the opposite, where the lower jaw sits further forward.
- **Labiomental fold:** the crease between your lower lip and chin. A deeper fold makes the chin look more prominent, and a shallow one makes it blend into the lip.

The chin-versus-jaw distinction matters. A chin that looks weak could come from the chin bone, the jaw position, the soft tissue over it, or simply how your nose and lips frame it. Those are different situations with different explanations.

## How Chin Projection Is Measured

There is no single universal test. A literature review of soft-tissue methods for judging chin projection, published in a Brazilian otorhinolaryngology journal, concluded that each method has its own particularities and none of them is complete or ideal. Keep that in mind whenever someone online quotes one number as *the* rule.

The common approaches look like this:

1. **Lower lip reference.** Drop an imaginary vertical line through the most forward point of the lower lip and see where the chin sits relative to it. This is the simplest and most widely quoted check.
2. **Facial angle.** This is a profile angle linking the forehead, the base of the nose and the chin. One clinical review of facial contouring lists an approximate aesthetic range of 165 to 175 degrees.
3. **Grading scales.** Clinicians and researchers use photo-based scales to rate chin retrusion consistently. The Croma Chin Projection Assessment Scale, described in a clinical trial registry entry, grades retrusion on five points, from ideal projection to very severe retrusion. A published clinical classification of microgenia also uses stages, with stage 0 as the ideal position of the soft tissue chin point.

These tools exist so that two clinicians looking at the same patient reach similar conclusions. They were not designed for grading your selfies, and they all depend on standardized photos taken under controlled conditions.

## Is There an Ideal Chin Projection?

The most quoted guideline appears in a [clinical review on cheek and chin contours](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11452099/). It states that chin projection should be approximately in line with the lower lip in men and 1 to 2 mm behind the lower lip in women.

That is a reasonable reference point. The fuller picture is more nuanced:

- **The rule started as an observation.** A paper analysing chin prominence relative to the lower lip notes that the guideline of not advancing the chin past the lower lip traces back to an *anecdotal* observation by a surgeon named Rosen. It is a useful starting point, not a law of nature.
- **Small differences register.** In a [study of facial profile preferences in Poland](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/CCIDE.S358452), respondents rated profile images of a young adult in which lip or chin position was shifted by 1 or 2 mm. Profiles with correct lip and chin position were rated highest, and scores dropped as retraction or protrusion increased. The authors concluded that a 1 mm change significantly altered the rankings.
- **The numbers depend on the population and the method.** In a study where 200 Chinese clinicians, dental students and orthognathic patients rated silhouette profiles, male chins were rated most attractive anywhere from 3 mm behind to 3 mm in front of the reference position. Female chins were rated most attractive at 3 mm forward. That study used a different reference from the lip-line rule, so its figures cannot be compared directly with the 1 to 2 mm guideline. That is exactly the point: the ideal moves with the measuring stick.
- **Features are judged together.** An Italian study found that observers tolerated nose protrusion more than a similar amount of chin protrusion. When the nose or chin protruded, slightly fuller lips improved profile ratings. Your chin is read in context with your nose and lips, never in isolation.
- **More is not automatically better.** An observational study of orthodontists, dentists and laypeople found that all three groups rated a retrusive chin as more attractive than a protrusive one. It was a small study, but it pushes back on the online idea that maximum projection is the goal.

**The takeaway:** there is a reasonable range rather than a single target. It differs by sex and by population, and how your chin looks depends heavily on the nose, lips and neck around it.

## How to Check Your Own Profile Without Fooling Yourself

Photos distort profiles more than most people realize. Head tilt alone can make a chin look either strong or recessed. If you want an honest look at your own chin projection, control the variables:

1. **Use the rear camera at head height.** Set the phone on a shelf or tripod at least an arm's length away, and use the timer. A front camera held close to the face exaggerates whatever is nearest the lens.
2. **Keep your head level.** Picture a line from the opening of your ear to the bottom of your eye socket, and keep it parallel to the floor. Tipping your head down pulls the chin back. Tipping it up pushes the chin forward.
3. **Relax your face.** Close your lips without pressing them, let your teeth touch lightly or rest slightly apart, and don't jut your jaw.
4. **Use soft, even light.** A window to your side or in front works well. Harsh overhead light deepens the fold under the lip and the shadow under the jaw.
5. **Take several shots and pick the most neutral.** Then look at where your chin sits relative to your lower lip, keeping the ranges above in mind.
6. **Note the context.** Look at the size of your nose, the fullness of your lips and the angle from chin to neck. These change the overall impression as much as the chin itself.

If you notice a *functional* issue, see a dentist or orthodontist for your health, not for appearance. Examples include teeth that don't meet evenly, jaw pain or clicking, and difficulty chewing.

## What Changes How Your Chin Reads, Without Medical Procedures

The suggestions below are **editorial styling and habit suggestions, not measured outcomes**. None of them changes bone. What they change is how the lower third of your face *reads*, in person and in photos.

### Posture and Head Position

In our editorial view, forward head posture, where the head drifts in front of the shoulders, softens the line between chin and neck. Standing tall with your head level gives a cleaner profile. In photos, photographers commonly ask people to extend the neck slightly and bring the face a touch forward rather than tucking the chin, because tucking creates folds under the jaw.

### Fullness Under the Chin

Soft tissue under the chin blurs the boundary between chin and neck, which makes projection harder to see. You can't spot-reduce fat from one area. If you are carrying extra weight, though, gradual overall fat loss through diet, activity and consistent sleep is the non-medical lever with the broadest effect on lower-face definition. That is an editorial observation, not a promise, because fat distribution varies widely between people.

### Beard Shaping

If you grow facial hair, the shape matters more than the length. Here is an editorial styling suggestion:

- Keep the beard **slightly longer and fuller at the chin** and shorter at the sides. This visually extends the chin forward and down.
- Keep a **clean neckline** roughly a finger or two above the Adam's apple, so the jaw-to-neck line stays crisp.
- Avoid leaving the sides fuller than the chin, which pulls the visual weight backward.

### Hair and Makeup

Hairstyles shape the whole profile, not just the top of the head. Volume at the crown or back of the head balances a prominent nose and a softer chin in side view. A low bun, a ponytail or hair tucked behind the ears shows the profile cleanly. With makeup, a soft highlight on the centre of the chin and light contour just under the jawline are common ways to bring the chin forward visually. These are style choices, so use only what fits how you like to look.

### Mewing and Chin Exercises

These are popular online. In the clinical literature we reviewed for this article, we found no evidence that tongue posture or chin exercises change the bony projection of an adult chin. Good posture is a fine habit for its own sake, but don't expect it to change your skeleton.

## What About Surgery and Fillers?

The procedures most often discussed are chin implants, sliding genioplasty (repositioning a cut segment of the chin bone) and filler injections. **We don't recommend chin implants, genioplasty or chin fillers for appearance.** The evidence highlights real risks and limits:

- **Results can shift over time.** A [systematic review comparing implants and bony genioplasty](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12262119/) reported relapse rates after sliding genioplasty ranging from 2.63% to 27.21% across studies.
- **Nerve sensation can be affected.** The chin area carries nerves that supply feeling to the lower lip. In one surgical series covering ten years of genioplasty, 93.1% of patients reported normal lower lip and chin sensation at least a year after surgery, which means some did not.
- **Implants change contour, not jaw position.** A surgical reference notes that chin implants do not reposition the genial tubercle (the bony point behind the chin where tongue muscles attach) and do not correct the jaw relationship.
- **Fillers are medical injections.** They carry their own risks, which only a doctor can explain for your situation.
- **Tiny changes have outsized effects.** Given the finding that a 1 mm shift changes how profiles are rated, a well-intended adjustment can easily overshoot.

Where a bite or jaw problem affects chewing, comfort or function, any treatment decision belongs to a dentist, orthodontist or oral surgeon treating that condition. **Talk to a qualified professional before considering this.**

## Practical Takeaways

- Chin projection is judged **relative to your lower lip and overall profile**, not in isolation.
- The widely quoted guideline is a chin roughly in line with the lower lip for men and 1 to 2 mm behind it for women. It is a *reference range*, not a pass or fail test.
- Measurement methods disagree with each other, and preferences vary by population. Also, a less projected chin was rated better than an overprojected one in at least one study.
- Before judging your chin, take a **controlled photo**: rear camera, level head, relaxed lips, soft light.
- The levers you control are posture, overall body fat, beard shape, hairstyle and makeup. They change how your chin reads, not its structure.
- Treat bite or jaw *function* problems as a health question for a dentist or orthodontist.
