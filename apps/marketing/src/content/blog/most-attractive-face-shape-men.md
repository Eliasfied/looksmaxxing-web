Search for the most attractive face shape for men and you will find a lot of confident answers. Square jaw. Oval is ideal. Diamond is rare and elite. Most of these lists read like settled science. They are not.

Academic studies of male attractiveness don't rank labels like oval, round or diamond. They measure **continuous traits**: how close a face is to the population average, how prominent the jaw is, how wide the face is relative to its height, and how masculine the overall shape reads. On several of these traits, the findings disagree with each other.

This guide covers what holds up, what doesn't, and why studies conflict. It ends with how to use your face shape where it does matter: haircuts, beards and glasses.

## The Short Answer: No Single Shape Wins

No study we found crowns one categorical face shape as the most attractive for men. The most consistent finding is not about a shape at all. It is about **averageness**, meaning how close a face's proportions sit to the typical face in a population.

The popular square-jaw ideal has mixed support. Some studies link masculine features to attractiveness. Others find no link, or even a negative one. One clearer pattern runs through the literature: **features that make a man look dominant are not always the features that make him look attractive.**

If someone tells you your face shape caps your attractiveness, they are claiming more certainty than the evidence gives.

## What Researchers Measure Instead of "Face Shapes"

The face shape categories you see online (oval, square, round, oblong, diamond, heart, triangle) are styling shorthand. Barbers and eyewear brands use them to pick cuts and frames. Researchers usually work with **facial landmarks**: dozens of points mapped across the brow, cheekbones, jaw and chin. They then analyze how shape varies statistically. This approach is called *geometric morphometrics*.

Three traits come up again and again.

### Averageness

In a study published in *Scientific Reports*, [averageness of face shape had a significant positive relationship with attractiveness ratings](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-86974-0). In the same analysis, masculinity of face shape was not significantly correlated with male attractiveness, and symmetry was not significantly correlated with attractiveness either.

Average does not mean bland. An averaged face tends to have balanced proportions with no single feature dominating. That balance can occur in an oval face, a square face or a rectangular one.

### Masculinity and the Jawline

*Sexual dimorphism* refers to the shape differences between typical male and female faces. Men's faces tend to have a more prominent brow ridge, a larger chin and a more defined jaw. The question is whether pushing a face further in the masculine direction makes it more attractive.

The evidence splits:

- In one study using a morphological measure of masculinity, [there was no relationship between morphological masculinity and rated attractiveness](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0013585). Skin colour, which the authors describe as a fluctuating, condition-dependent cue, did predict attractiveness.
- A facial anthropometry study found that, in both sexes, [facial attractiveness was negatively associated with facial masculinity](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p7214), and that preference was not associated with symmetry.
- A different study found that [male attractiveness was positively predicted by masculinity, symmetry and averageness](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep39731).
- A conjoint study found that [eyebrow thickness and jawbone prominence mattered more to overall attractiveness than cheekbone prominence and eye size](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6290027/). A masculine jaw was rated more attractive on male faces than on female faces.

So a defined jaw can count in your favor in some study designs. It is not a universal rule.

### Face Width: The fWHR Question

The *facial width-to-height ratio* (fWHR) is [the width of the face divided by the height of the upper face](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4504483/). Width is measured across the cheekbones. Height runs roughly from the brow to the upper lip. It has been one of the most studied face metrics.

The same meta-analysis found that [individuals with larger fWHRs were judged as less attractive, especially when women made the judgments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4504483/).

A speed-dating study complicates this. Men with higher fWHR were rated as [more dominant, were more likely to be chosen for a second date, and were rated more attractive for short-term relationships](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24458269/). A later study found that [fWHR was not reliably linked to raters' judgments of male faces for any of the 13 trait inferences tested](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513821000568).

The practical reading: a wider face reads as more dominant in some studies. That does not reliably translate into being seen as more attractive.

## Why the Studies Disagree

If one study says masculine faces are more attractive and another says they are less attractive, method is one likely reason.

### Edited Faces vs. Natural Faces

Many studies took one face and digitally morphed it to look more or less masculine. The *Scientific Reports* authors point out that studies finding masculinity effects [typically used stimuli in which shape characteristics were experimentally manipulated](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-86974-0). They suggest this may help explain conflicting results. But measuring natural variation does not settle the question: one study found [masculinity positively predicted male attractiveness](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep39731), while others found no link or a negative one.

A review paper makes a related point. It [re-evaluates claims](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11184995/) that women see masculine-faced men as less likely to invest in relationships, partly on methodological grounds.

### Dominance Is Not Attractiveness

One of the steadier findings is that masculine features and attractiveness are separate dimensions:

- A combination of masculine features, including a square jaw and thick eyebrows, [raised dominance ratings of male faces](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513801000666).
- In a geometric morphometrics study, [male physical strength was more strongly associated with face shapes linked to perceived masculinity and dominance than with attractiveness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21957062/). Highly attractive men in that sample tended toward longer, narrower face shapes rather than the broadest ones.
- A meta-analysis found [a small but significant positive relationship between men's fWHR and aggression](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4388848/). That is a weak link, and it is not a feature most people are looking for in a partner.

Many online rankings blend "looks tough" with "looks attractive." The research treats them as different things.

### Context and Who Is Rating

Preferences shift with context, such as short-term vs. long-term interest, as the speed-dating result shows. Facial cues of muscle mass in men [predict how masculine women perceive a face to be](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7156615/), but perceived masculinity is not the same as attractiveness. Samples also vary by country, age and rater group, so a result from one population may not transfer to another.

## Where the Square-Jaw Ideal Comes From

The idea that square faces are best has some real data behind it, but the context matters.

An AI analysis of male celebrity faces found that [square or trapezoidal facial shapes were perceived as more attractive across most ethnic groups, with Asian populations tending to favor a slightly more triangular outline](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2468785526000728). These were faces the media had already deemed attractive. That tells you about the media standard, not about what ordinary raters prefer in everyday life.

The ideal also shows up in how men see themselves. In one study, [men favored a well-defined chin, flatter cheeks and cheekbones, and more pronounced brow ridges, nose and mid-forehead](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0245557) when rating their own attractiveness. That measures self-perception, not how others rate you.

A separate study of 208 men looked at external ratings. It found that [attractive male faces tended to show an angular, well-defined structure, less fullness at the sides and slightly greater lower-face height](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40510-026-00617-2). Read the word *tended*: this describes a pattern in one sample, not a requirement.

Put together, there is a recurring preference for definition and balance. That is a long way from "square beats oval."

## What Actually Changes How Your Face Reads

Your bone structure is mostly fixed. How it comes across is not. These are **editorial suggestions** about presentation, not measured attractiveness effects:

1. **Skin condition.** In the morphological masculinity study, skin colour was a significant predictor of attractiveness, and the authors describe it as a fluctuating, condition-dependent cue. A consistent basic routine of cleanser, moisturizer and sunscreen is a reasonable place to start.
2. **Haircut proportions.** Volume on top lengthens a face. Volume at the sides widens it. A good barber uses this deliberately.
3. **Facial hair as framing.** A beard can visually extend a short chin or sharpen a soft jawline. Clean edges at the neckline and cheek line matter more than length.
4. **Eyebrow grooming.** Eyebrow thickness was one of the more salient features in the conjoint study. Tidy strays, but don't over-thin.
5. **Glasses and frame shape.** Frames add lines to your face. Picking frames that contrast with your face shape is a long-standing eyewear convention.

### Getting a Useful Read at Home

If you'd rather check manually:

1. Pull your hair back and take a straight-on photo at eye level, in even light, with a neutral expression.
2. Compare the widths of your forehead, cheekbones and jaw. Which is widest?
3. Compare overall face length to cheekbone width. Is it clearly longer, or close to equal?
4. Look at your jaw angle. Is it sharp and defined, or rounded?
5. Match the pattern to the closest row in the table. Then bring the photo to your barber and ask what they see.

## Myths to Drop

- **"Oval is scientifically the best shape."** We found no study ranking shape labels.
- **"Women universally prefer a square jaw."** Some studies find null or negative effects for masculinity, while others find positive ones.
- **"A wider face makes you more attractive."** The meta-analytic evidence leans the other way. The positive speed-dating result was limited to short-term contexts.
- **"Symmetry is everything."** Several of the studies above found no significant symmetry effect.
- **"Celebrity ratios are a target."** Proportions measured on media-selected faces describe a media standard, not a goal for your face.

## The Bottom Line

There is no single most attractive face shape for men. The research points to balance and averageness over any one outline. A defined jaw counts in your favor in some studies and not in others. A wide, dominant-looking face is not the advantage it is often sold as.

The useful part is practical. Learn your likely face shape, use it to pick haircuts, beard lines and frames, and take care of the condition-dependent things like skin. That is a better use of your energy than worrying about a ranking the evidence doesn't support.
