If you've spent any time in fitness or self-improvement spaces online, you've almost certainly encountered the word **mewing**. At first it looks like a meme. Then you dig a little deeper and find orthodontists, speech therapists, and researchers actually discussing the underlying mechanics. So what's real, what's exaggerated, and how do you actually do it correctly?

This guide covers everything: the mewing definition, the correct technique, realistic expectations, and the science behind it.

## What Is Mewing? The Actual Definition

**Mewing** is the practice of resting your entire tongue flat against the roof of your mouth (the palate) as your default resting posture. The term comes from Dr. Mike Mew, a British orthodontist who popularized his father Dr. John Mew's theory of *orthotropics*, which argues that oral posture, chewing forces, and breathing habits shape the development of the facial skeleton over time.

The mewing definition in plain terms: instead of letting your tongue sit at the bottom of your mouth or pressed behind your front teeth, you place the whole tongue, including the back third, against the palate and breathe through your nose.

That's it. No device, no surgery, no special exercise. Just a resting posture.

### Why It Became a Meme

The mewing meme spread because before-and-after photos circulated online showing dramatic jawline changes attributed to the practice. Some of those photos were legitimate; many were lighting changes, weight loss, or simply puberty. The mewing face meme typically shows someone doing an exaggerated jaw clench with hollow cheeks, which is not actually mewing and misrepresents the technique entirely.

Beneath the mewing face meme, though, the core idea has a real basis in craniofacial biology. That's worth understanding.

## The Science Behind Mewing

The foundational claim of mewing rests on a property of bone called **mechanotransduction**: bone tissue remodels in response to sustained mechanical forces. The palate is not a fixed vault. It's made of two halves (the maxillary palatine processes) joined at the intermaxillary suture, and that suture remains at least partially responsive to pressure well into adulthood in many individuals.

When the tongue rests against the palate, it exerts a gentle, constant upward and forward force. The hypothesis is that this force, over months and years, may encourage the maxilla (the upper jaw bone) to develop forward and laterally rather than downward and backward.

**Research suggests** a link between low tongue posture, mouth breathing, and a longer, narrower facial structure, particularly in children. Studies on nasal versus oral breathing in growing animals show measurable differences in midface development. Human epidemiological data supports a similar pattern.

For adults, the evidence is less clear. Bone remodeling slows significantly after the growth plates close, typically in the late teens to early twenties. Some researchers argue that sutures remain biologically active longer than previously thought. Others are more skeptical. The honest position: the research is genuinely incomplete.

What is less contested is that **correct tongue posture may reduce strain on the temporomandibular joint (TMJ)**, support better nasal breathing, and improve the resting tone of the muscles around the jaw and chin.

## Mewing Tutorial: How to Do It Correctly

Most people get the technique wrong at first. Here's the step-by-step approach.

### Step 1: Find the Correct Tongue Position

1. Make a "clicking" sound with the tip of your tongue against the roof of your mouth, just behind your front teeth. Notice where your tongue tip lands. That's roughly where it should rest.
2. Now, without swallowing, try to suction the **entire tongue** to the roof of your mouth, not just the tip. The back third of the tongue is the part most people forget, and it's arguably the most important for palate pressure.
3. Your lips should be closed, teeth lightly touching or very slightly apart (not clenched), and you should be breathing through your nose.

### Step 2: Check Your Swallowing Pattern

The way you swallow matters. A **proper swallow** (sometimes called a "mewing swallow") involves the tongue pressing up against the palate and propelling the bolus back, with no lip strain. An improper swallow often involves the tongue pushing forward against the teeth. If you notice your lips straining or your front teeth getting pushed when you swallow, that's a sign of a tongue thrust pattern worth addressing.

### Step 3: Build the Habit

Mewing only does anything if it becomes your resting default. That requires consistent repetition over a long period.

- **Set reminders** for the first few weeks until it feels natural.
- Check your posture during activities where you're likely to forget: watching a screen, working out, reading.
- Many people find the back third of the tongue falls away from the palate unconsciously. Focus on keeping that contact.

Expect some mild tongue fatigue in the first week or two. That's normal, and it reflects muscles being asked to hold a new position.

## Mewing Exercises to Support the Practice

Mewing itself is a passive posture, not an active exercise. But there are supporting **mewing exercises** that may strengthen the relevant muscles and reinforce correct patterns.

### Chewing Harder Foods

The masseter (the thick jaw muscle you can feel when you clench) and the pterygoid muscles develop with use. Regularly chewing fibrous, tough foods, such as raw vegetables, tougher cuts of meat, or dedicated chewing gum formulated for jaw exercise, provides a mechanical stimulus that soft, processed diets don't.

### Tongue-to-Palate Suction Hold

Suction your entire tongue to the palate as hard as you can and hold for 10 to 30 seconds. Release slowly. Repeat 10 times. This builds awareness of full tongue contact and strengthens the muscles involved. Some people call this a mewing exercise or a tongue suction drill.

### Nasal Breathing Drills

If you default to mouth breathing, mewing is difficult to maintain. Practice deliberate nasal breathing during low-intensity activities. Some people use mouth tape during sleep as an aid, though you should consult a doctor before doing that if you have any nasal obstruction or sleep-disordered breathing.

## What to Realistically Expect: Mewing Before and After

This is where honesty matters most.

**Children and teenagers** going through active facial development have the most to gain. Correct tongue posture during growth phases may genuinely influence the trajectory of jaw and midface development. This is where orthotropics has its strongest theoretical and clinical support.

**Adults** should have more moderate expectations. The most commonly reported benefits among adult practitioners are:

- Improved definition in the jawline and neck area due to better muscle tone and posture
- Reduced TMJ discomfort in some cases
- Better nasal breathing, which has its own downstream benefits for sleep quality and energy
- Subtle improvement in facial symmetry and forward projection over extended periods (measured in years, not weeks)

The dramatic mewing before and after transformations you see online are almost always either adolescents who mewed during peak growth, or photos that conflate weight loss, improved lighting, and better grooming with mewing specifically. Some users report slow, gradual changes in jawline definition over 1 to 2 years of consistent practice. Others report no visible change at all.

## Common Mewing Mistakes

These are the errors that undermine results or cause discomfort.

- **Only raising the tongue tip.** The back third of the tongue is the part that applies the most pressure to the palate. Raising only the front is partial technique.
- **Teeth clenching.** Mewing is not about clenching the mewing jaw together. The teeth should be lightly in contact or slightly apart. Chronic clenching can worsen TMJ issues.
- **Asymmetric tongue placement.** Consistently placing more tongue pressure on one side may reinforce facial asymmetry rather than reduce it. Aim for even, full contact.
- **Expecting fast results.** Bone remodeling, if it occurs, happens over years. Soft tissue changes like muscle tone take months. Checking weekly for changes will only frustrate you.
- **Using a mewing device as a shortcut.** Various mewing devices are sold online claiming to automate tongue posture training. Most are not clinically validated. A few tongue trainers have some physical therapy backing, but none replace developing the habit organically. Approach any mewing device with healthy skepticism and check credentials carefully.

## Practical Tips for Staying Consistent

- **Pair it with an existing habit.** Every time you sit down at your desk or pick up your phone, do a quick tongue posture check.
- **Fix your overall posture first.** Forward head posture makes correct tongue posture much harder to maintain. Work on neck and upper back strength alongside mewing.
- **Breathe through your nose during exercise.** Nasal breathing during light to moderate exercise trains the habit under mild stress.
- **Be patient with asymmetry.** Most people have some tongue-side dominance. It takes deliberate effort to balance.
- **Document your progress with consistent conditions.** Same lighting, same angle, same time of day, ideally measured with objective tools rather than subjective mirror checks.
